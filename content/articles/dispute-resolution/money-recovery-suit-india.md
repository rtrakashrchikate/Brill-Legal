A money claim is only as valuable as its ability to translate into actual recovery. Winning a suit and obtaining a decree is the first half of the task; enforcing it against a resisting defendant is the second, and often the harder one. Understanding the full arc of civil money recovery, from forum selection through judgment to execution, is essential to any sensible recovery strategy.

## When to Use Civil Courts for Money Recovery

Civil courts are the primary forum for recovering debts, unpaid invoices, loan amounts, damages for breach of contract, and other liquidated or quantifiable claims. The route chosen within the civil court system depends on the nature of the underlying obligation and the characteristics of the defendant.

For commercial disputes above the pecuniary threshold set by the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, the designated Commercial Court offers case management, strict timelines, and summary judgment provisions that are not available in ordinary civil courts. For disputes below that threshold, the Civil Court (Junior or Senior Division) is the appropriate forum. Lok Adalats offer a settlement-based route for smaller or partly disputed amounts, producing an award that is equivalent to a decree and cannot be appealed.

## Regular Suit

A regular civil suit under the CPC is the standard route for money claims. The plaintiff files a plaint, the defendant files a written statement, issues are framed, evidence is led by both parties, and the court delivers a judgment from which a formal decree is drawn. The procedure is thorough and gives both sides a full opportunity to present their case.

The limitation of the regular suit in money recovery matters is that a defendant who has no genuine defence but wishes to delay payment can use each stage of the procedure to string out the timeline. Filing a written statement late, seeking repeated adjournments at the evidence stage, and challenging procedural steps in revision are all tactics that lengthen ordinary civil proceedings significantly.

## Summary Suit Under Order XXXVII CPC

Order XXXVII CPC provides a faster track for specific categories of debt. The core mechanism is the reversal of the ordinary burden: in a summary suit, the defendant does not have the automatic right to file a written statement. Instead, the defendant must first obtain leave to defend from the court by demonstrating a genuine triable issue. If leave is refused, a decree is entered immediately.

Summary suits are available for bills of exchange, hundis, and promissory notes, and for suits where the plaintiff seeks recovery of a debt or liquidated demand arising on a written contract, an enactment, or a guarantee. A liquidated demand is one that is fixed and ascertainable on the face of the document, as distinct from an unliquidated claim for damages that requires quantification.

When the defendant applies for leave to defend, the court assesses the quality of the defence. Where the defence appears genuine and raises a real triable issue, unconditional leave is granted and the suit proceeds on the ordinary track. Where the defence is doubtful or conditional, the court may grant leave subject to the defendant depositing a sum as security. Where the defence is clearly sham or untenable, leave is refused and the decree enters immediately. The summary suit is most effective where the underlying document is unambiguous and the defendant's only response is a bare denial or a request for time.

## Which Debts Qualify for Summary Procedure

A key step before filing is confirming that the debt qualifies for Order XXXVII. Not every money claim does. The claim must arise from one of the specified instruments or from a written contract for a liquidated sum. Claims for damages arising from breach of contract, claims where the quantum is disputed and requires assessment, and claims in tort do not fall within Order XXXVII and must proceed by way of a regular suit.

Unpaid invoices acknowledged in writing, outstanding loan repayments under signed loan agreements, and rent arrears under a written lease with a specified rent amount are typical examples of debts that qualify. The strength of the plaintiff's position in a summary suit is directly proportional to the clarity and completeness of the underlying documents.

## Attachment Before Judgment

Where there is a risk that the defendant will dissipate assets before the court can pass a decree, Order XXXVIII Rule 5 CPC provides a powerful protective remedy. A plaintiff may apply for attachment before judgment by showing that the defendant is about to dispose of property, remove property from the jurisdiction, or otherwise deal with assets in a manner intended to obstruct or delay the execution of a likely decree.

This is a drastic remedy: it freezes the defendant's assets before any finding of liability. Courts require clear and cogent evidence of the defendant's intention to dissipate assets, not merely the plaintiff's suspicion or apprehension. Where the plaintiff can demonstrate such intention, however, attachment before judgment can be decisive, preventing the gap between filing and decree from being used by the defendant to render any eventual decree worthless.

## Garnishee Proceedings

After a decree is obtained, if the judgment-debtor is themselves owed money by a third party, a garnishee order provides a direct route to recovery. The garnishee is a third party who owes money to the judgment-debtor, such as the judgment-debtor's bank holding funds in an account, or a trade debtor of the judgment-debtor. The court can issue a garnishee order directing that third party to pay the relevant sum to the decree-holder instead of to the judgment-debtor.

Garnishee proceedings are initiated in the execution stage. The court issues a preliminary order calling on the garnishee to show cause why the attachment should not be made final. If the garnishee does not appear or does not contest, the order is made absolute and the garnishee must pay the decree-holder directly.

## Decree Execution

Once a decree is obtained, execution is commenced by filing an execution petition before the court that passed the decree or a court to which it has been transferred. Execution remedies available to the decree-holder include attachment and sale of the judgment-debtor's moveable and immoveable property, delivery of possession of property where the decree is for possession, appointment of a court receiver to manage assets, and in limited circumstances prescribed by Section 51 CPC, the arrest of the judgment-debtor.

The execution court has broad investigative powers. It can examine the judgment-debtor under oath about their assets, compel the production of documents, and hold the judgment-debtor in contempt for non-compliance with an examination order. Under Article 136 of the Limitation Act, an execution petition must be filed within twelve years of the decree (or three years for certain categories), making timely action important.

## Practical Considerations

Recovery litigation rewards advance preparation. Before filing, investigating the defendant's actual asset position is as important as assessing the legal merits of the claim. Searching property registration records, the Ministry of Corporate Affairs database for company directorships and financial filings, and the CERSAI registry for encumbrances on immoveable property helps identify what assets will be available to satisfy a decree. A decree obtained against a defendant with no reachable assets, or whose assets are heavily encumbered, offers limited practical value regardless of its legal validity.

A money claim is only as useful as its ability to translate into actual recovery. Winning a suit and obtaining a decree is the first step; enforcing it against a resisting defendant is the second, and often the harder one. Understanding the full arc — from forum selection through judgment to execution — is essential to any sensible recovery strategy.

## Choosing the route

### Regular suit
A regular civil suit under the CPC is the standard route for money claims. The plaintiff files a plaint, the defendant files a written statement, issues are framed, evidence is led, and the court delivers judgment. It is thorough, but it can be slow — particularly for debts where the defendant's real intention is delay rather than a genuine defence.

### Summary suit (Order XXXVII)
For certain categories of debt — bills of exchange, hundis, promissory notes, and written contracts for a liquidated (fixed) sum — Order XXXVII provides a streamlined procedure. The defendant cannot file a defence as of right; they must first obtain **leave to defend** from the court. If leave is refused, a decree is entered immediately. If leave is granted, the suit proceeds on the ordinary track, but with the plaintiff already having demonstrated a strong case. Summary suits are discussed in detail in our [guide to Order XXXVII](/insights/summary-suit-order-37).

### Commercial courts
For commercial disputes of a specified value (currently one lakh rupees and above in most states), the Commercial Courts Act 2015 provides a dedicated forum with strict timelines, active case management, and summary judgment provisions. For business-to-business recovery, the commercial court is usually the right venue.

### Lok Adalat
For undisputed or partly-disputed smaller debts, the Lok Adalat system offers an award that is deemed a decree and cannot be appealed. Settlement at a Lok Adalat is final.

## Attachment before judgment

Order XXXVIII CPC allows a plaintiff to apply for attachment of the defendant's property *before* a decree is obtained, where the court is satisfied that the defendant:

- is about to dispose of property with intent to obstruct or delay the execution of a likely decree, or
- is about to leave the jurisdiction with the same intent

This is a drastic remedy — essentially freezing someone's assets before any finding of liability — and courts require clear evidence of the defendant's intention, not merely the plaintiff's apprehension. But where it is available, it can be decisive: it prevents dissipation of assets in the gap between filing and judgment.

## Garnishee orders

Where the defendant is owed money by a third party (a bank, a debtor of their own), a garnishee order can be obtained attaching that sum in the third party's hands. This is an execution-stage tool, available after a decree is obtained. The garnishee is summoned to show cause why the amount should not be attached and paid to the decree-holder.

## The execution process

Once a decree is obtained, execution is commenced by filing an execution petition before the same court (or a court to which the decree is transferred). The decree-holder may seek:

- Attachment and sale of the judgment-debtor's moveable or immoveable property
- Delivery of possession of property
- Arrest of the judgment-debtor (in restricted circumstances under Section 51 CPC — generally not for ordinary money decrees)
- Appointment of a court receiver to manage assets

The execution court has wide powers. It can examine the judgment-debtor about their assets, compel disclosure, and hold them in contempt for non-compliance.

## Practical realities

Recovery litigation rewards preparation. Before filing, it is worth investigating the defendant's actual asset position — searching property records, the Ministry of Corporate Affairs database for company directorships, and the CERSAI registry for encumbrances. A decree is worthless if the defendant has nothing, or if what they have is out of reach.

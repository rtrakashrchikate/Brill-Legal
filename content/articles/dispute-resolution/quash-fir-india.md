An FIR registered against a person sets in motion a criminal process that is, by design, difficult to halt. The police have broad investigative powers, and the prosecution machinery has its own momentum. Yet not every FIR deserves to run its full course, and Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS) preserves the High Court's inherent power to prevent abuse of the process of court and to secure the ends of justice. Quashing a bad FIR is among the most direct applications of that power.

## The Legal Basis: Section 528 BNSS

Section 528 BNSS replaced Section 482 of the Code of Criminal Procedure and carries forward the same inherent jurisdiction of the High Court. The provision is not exhaustively defined: it is a residual power to be exercised where no other remedy is adequate. Courts approach it with caution and exercise it sparingly, but they do exercise it, and in a meaningful range of situations.

The Supreme Court in State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal (1992) laid down the categories of cases where quashing is appropriate. These categories have been applied, refined, and expanded in the decades since, and they remain the governing framework. The categories include situations where the allegations in the FIR, taken at face value, do not disclose a cognisable offence; where the FIR is frivolous or motivated by malice; where the allegations are inherently improbable or contradicted by unimpeachable evidence on the record; where the offence is private in nature and the parties have genuinely settled; and where continuing the prosecution would itself amount to an abuse of process.

## High Court Jurisdiction

The High Court of the state where the FIR was registered has jurisdiction under Section 528 BNSS. Both the Bombay High Court and its benches at Aurangabad, Nagpur, and Goa exercise this power for FIRs registered in Maharashtra. The Supreme Court exercises a parallel power under Article 136 and Article 142 of the Constitution in exceptional cases, particularly where constitutional rights are engaged or where the matter involves multiple states.

It is important to note that the jurisdiction is not appellate: the High Court does not retry facts or re-examine the investigation in detail. It looks at the FIR, the complaint, and the material placed before it and asks whether continuing the proceedings would serve any legitimate purpose. The threshold for interference is not low, but where the case falls clearly within the Bhajan Lal categories, the High Court's power is well-established.

## Grounds for Quashing

### No Offence Disclosed on the Face of the FIR

The most straightforward ground is that the FIR, read generously and accepting every allegation as true, does not disclose a cognisable offence under the BNS or any other penal statute. If the facts alleged, even if proved, would not constitute a crime in law, there is no purpose in investigation proceeding. This is a pure legal question, which makes it suitable for determination at the High Court level without a full trial.

### Civil Dispute Converted into a Criminal Case

Commercial disputes, contract failures, landlord-tenant conflicts, and business partnership disagreements do not become criminal matters simply because a complainant uses criminal language in the FIR. Courts look through the description to the substance of the grievance. Where the core of the dispute is about money, property, or contractual performance, and where the criminal ingredient, namely dishonest or fraudulent intent at the inception of the transaction, is absent from the facts alleged, the FIR is vulnerable to quashing.

The distinction drawn by courts is between a transaction that was entered into honestly and later went wrong, which is a civil matter, and a transaction entered into with the intention from the outset to cheat or defraud, which is the criminal ingredient under Section 318 BNS (cheating). Where the FIR does not allege facts pointing to the latter, the High Court may quash.

### Matrimonial and Family Disputes with Settlement

Complaints under Section 85 BNS (cruelty to a wife) and related matrimonial offences are among the most common subjects of quashing petitions, particularly when the parties reach a mutual settlement as part of a consent divorce or separation agreement. Courts regularly quash such FIRs where both parties jointly approach the court confirming a genuine settlement and seeking relief. The court evaluates whether the settlement is voluntary, free from coercion, and whether the complainant is not being pressured to withdraw.

### Malafide or Motivated Complaints

Where there is material on record suggesting that the FIR was filed not to seek justice but to harass, coerce, or gain leverage in a civil or commercial dispute, the High Court may quash. Courts are careful here: the threshold for establishing malafide intent is not low, and assertions of malice alone, without supporting material, are insufficient. However, where the timing of the FIR, the identity of the complainant, and the sequence of events point clearly to an ulterior motive, quashing has been granted.

## Cases Where Courts Decline to Quash

Understanding where courts will not intervene is as important as knowing where they will. The High Court consistently declines to quash FIRs in cases involving offences against society at large, such as murder, rape, organised crime, and terrorism, where there is no question of private settlement affecting the public interest. Economic offences with wide societal impact, including large-scale bank fraud, securities fraud, and offences under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, are treated similarly: courts take the position that public interest outweighs any private compromise.

Courts are also reluctant to intervene where investigation is at an early stage and the full picture has not emerged. Quashing is not a substitute for the investigation process, and courts hesitate to foreclose an inquiry before it has run its course. Where the settlement produced before the court does not appear genuine, particularly where there is evidence of coercion or where the terms are not reflected in a free and voluntary agreement, the court will not act on it.

## The Quashing Process

A petition under Section 528 BNSS is filed before the High Court, naming the state as a respondent alongside the original complainant. On admission, the court may issue a direction that no coercive steps be taken against the petitioner, providing practical interim protection while the petition is heard.

The police file a response on behalf of the state. The individual complainant may also file a reply. The matter is then argued before a single judge. Where settlement is the basis for the petition, affidavits from both the petitioner and the complainant confirming the settlement are placed before the court. In matrimonial matters especially, courts scrutinise these affidavits carefully to ensure the agreement is genuine and the complainant is not being pressured.

## Timing and Strategic Considerations

The stage at which a quashing petition is filed has a direct bearing on its prospects. Courts are more willing to intervene during investigation, before a chargesheet is filed, than after the case has progressed to trial. Once a chargesheet is filed and cognisance is taken, the threshold for quashing rises: the court must be satisfied that proceeding to trial would itself be an abuse of process, not merely that the case is weak. Once a trial is underway, quashing becomes still more exceptional.

The practical implication is that if quashing is a realistic option in a given case, it should be pursued promptly rather than after waiting to see how the investigation develops. Delay in approaching the court, particularly where the petitioner has been participating in investigation without objection, can be held against them on the question of whether the petition is bona fide.

An FIR sets a criminal process in motion that is, by design, difficult to stop. The police have wide investigation powers and the prosecution machinery has its own momentum. But not every FIR deserves to run its full course. Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita gives the High Court inherent powers to prevent the abuse of the court's process — and quashing a bad FIR is among the most direct uses of that power.

## The legal basis for quashing

Section 528 BNSS (which replaced Section 482 CrPC) preserves the High Court's inherent power to make such orders as may be necessary to prevent abuse of process or to secure the ends of justice. This is a broad jurisdiction, and courts use it sparingly — but they do use it.

The Supreme Court has laid down the grounds for quashing in a series of decisions, most authoritatively in *State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal* (1992), which remains the leading case. The categories identified there — and expanded over the decades — include:

- The allegations in the FIR, even if taken at face value, do not disclose any cognisable offence
- The FIR is manifestly frivolous, motivated by malice, or filed as an instrument of pressure in a civil dispute
- The allegations are so inherently improbable or contradicted by unimpeachable evidence that no reasonable court should allow the prosecution to continue
- The offence is primarily private in nature and the parties have genuinely settled
- Continuing the prosecution would amount to an abuse of process

## Cases that courts will typically quash

### Disputes dressed as crimes

A commercial dispute — a contract gone wrong, a failed business partnership, a landlord-tenant conflict — does not become a criminal matter simply because the complainant describes it using criminal language. Courts look through the pleadings to the substance. Where the core grievance is about money or property rather than criminal intent, the FIR is vulnerable.

This does not mean every civil dispute is immune from criminal investigation. Where a complaint genuinely alleges cheating under Section 318 BNS (criminal dishonesty at the inception of a transaction), courts will not quash merely because a civil remedy also exists. The question is whether the criminal ingredient — mens rea, dishonest intent — is actually present on the facts alleged.

### Matrimonial and family disputes

Section 85 BNS (cruelty to a wife) and related matrimonial offences are frequently the subject of quashing petitions when the parties reach a mutual settlement, often as part of a consent divorce. Courts regularly quash such FIRs where a settlement has been reached and both parties seek the relief jointly, treating the continuation of prosecution as unnecessary and harmful.

### No offence disclosed on the face of the FIR

If the FIR itself, read generously, does not disclose a cognisable offence, there is no point in an investigation proceeding. This is the most straightforward ground — the court reads the FIR as if everything stated in it is true, and asks whether even that amounts to a crime. If not, the FIR goes.

## Cases that courts will not quash

Courts consistently decline to quash FIRs in:

- **Serious offences against society** — murder, rape, organised crime, terrorism — where there is no question of private settlement
- **Economic offences** with wide impact — bank fraud, securities fraud, PMLA matters — where courts take the view that public interest overrides private compromise
- **Cases where investigation is ongoing and the material is yet to emerge** — courts are cautious about acting before the investigation has run its course
- **Cases where the settlement is not genuine** — particularly where there is evidence of coercion or where the settlement papers do not reflect a free and mutual agreement

## The quashing process

A petition under Section 528 BNSS is filed before the High Court of the state where the FIR was registered. The petition names the state as a respondent (the police are investigating on the state's behalf) and typically the original complainant as well.

On admission, the court may direct that no coercive steps be taken against the petitioner — functioning in practice as interim protection. The police file their response; where the complainant is an individual, they may also file a reply. The matter is then argued before a single judge of the High Court.

If the matter settles and the settlement is relied upon, an affidavit from both the original complainant and the petitioner confirming the settlement is placed before the court. Courts scrutinise these carefully in matrimonial and family matters to ensure the settlement is genuine.

## Timing and strategy

The stage at which a quashing petition is filed affects its prospects. Courts are more willing to intervene before or during investigation than after a chargesheet has been filed and a trial has commenced. Once a chargesheet is filed, the threshold rises: the court must be satisfied that allowing the trial to continue would itself be an abuse of process.

This means that if quashing is a realistic option, it should be pursued promptly — not after waiting through investigation for evidence to pile up. The corollary is that if the matter is complex or the facts are in genuine dispute, quashing is not always the right strategy; bail and vigorous defence at trial may be more appropriate.

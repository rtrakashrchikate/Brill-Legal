Arrest is the sharpest power the state exercises over a citizen, and the law fences it with safeguards at every step — before arrest, at the moment of arrest, and in the hours after. Most violations happen because the arrested person and their family do not know the rules. This guide sets them out plainly. It belongs to our [criminal defence practice](/practices/dispute-resolution), alongside our [anticipatory bail guide](/insights/anticipatory-bail-india-guide).

## Before arrest: arrest is the exception, not the rule

For offences punishable with up to seven years' imprisonment, the law (Section 35 BNSS, carrying forward *Arnesh Kumar*'s mandate) requires police to ask whether arrest is actually **necessary** — to prevent further offences, to secure evidence, to prevent witness tampering, or to ensure court appearance. If not, the officer must issue a **notice of appearance** instead, and you comply by appearing as directed.

Practical meaning: in the vast run of cases — matrimonial complaints, business disputes recast as cheating cases, neighbourhood quarrels — immediate arrest is improper, and a magistrate authorising detention must check that the officer recorded valid reasons. Courts take *Arnesh Kumar* compliance seriously, and its breach is a strong ground in bail proceedings.

## At the moment of arrest: your core rights

**Grounds of arrest, in writing.** You have a constitutional right (Article 22) to be informed of the grounds. The Supreme Court — in *Pankaj Bansal* (ED arrests) and *Prabir Purkayastha*, now extended to ordinary police arrests — requires grounds to be furnished **in writing**. An arrest without communicated grounds is open to being declared illegal.

**The arrest memo.** Under the *D.K. Basu* guidelines, now statutory, the arresting officer must prepare a memo recording time, date and place of arrest, attested by a family member or respectable local witness and countersigned by you.

**Identification.** Officers must bear visible, accurate name tags; the personnel handling the interrogation must be recorded.

**Information to family.** The police must inform a relative or friend of your arrest and where you are held — and a control-room entry of the arrest must be made.

**No unnecessary restraint.** Handcuffing is the exception, requiring recorded justification — not the default.

## After arrest: the first 24 hours

**Production before a magistrate within 24 hours** (excluding journey time) is an absolute rule under Article 22(2). Detention beyond it without a remand order is illegal custody, remediable by habeas corpus.

**Right to counsel.** You may consult a lawyer of your choice and have them present **during interrogation** (though not throughout every moment of questioning). If you cannot afford one, free legal aid is a right, not charity — the magistrate must inform you of it.

**Medical examination.** You are entitled to be medically examined at arrest and every 48 hours in custody — the record protects you against custodial violence and documents any injuries.

**Right to silence.** No arrested person can be compelled to be a witness against themselves (Article 20(3)). You must provide identification details, but you cannot be forced to confess — and confessions to police are inadmissible in any event.

**Women's safeguards.** Arrest only by or in the presence of a woman officer; no arrest after sunset and before sunrise save in exceptional circumstances with a magistrate's prior permission; searches only by women with strict decency.

## Remand, bail and what happens next

At first production, the magistrate decides custody: **police custody** (limited to the statutory window) or **judicial custody**. This hearing matters — it is where illegal-arrest objections, *Arnesh Kumar* non-compliance and medical complaints go on record. The bail application follows: as of right in bailable offences, on the court's discretion otherwise. If you anticipated this moment, an [anticipatory bail order](/insights/anticipatory-bail-india-guide) converts arrest into immediate release.

In agency matters — ED, EOW, SFIO — the same constitutional architecture applies with statute-specific overlays; written grounds of arrest have been most fiercely litigated precisely in [white-collar cases](/insights/white-collar-crime-india-guide).

## If rights are violated

- **Habeas corpus** before the High Court for illegal detention
- **Bail arguments** — procedural violations weigh heavily for release
- **Trial consequences** — illegally obtained custodial statements lose evidentiary force
- **Compensation** — constitutional courts award monetary compensation for established custodial violations
- **Accountability** — contempt and departmental action against violating officers under *D.K. Basu*

## A one-paragraph checklist for families

Note the time and place of arrest; ask for the grounds in writing and the police station; ensure the arrest memo was attested; call a lawyer immediately — before the first interrogation, not after; attend the magistrate production within 24 hours; and insist the medical examination is done and recorded. Those six steps preserve nearly every right that matters.

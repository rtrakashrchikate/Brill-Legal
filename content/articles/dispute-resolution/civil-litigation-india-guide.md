Civil litigation covers the disputes that affect everyday life and business — unpaid dues, broken contracts, property and inheritance, and the protection of legal rights. Unlike a criminal case, which the State brings to punish an offence, a civil suit is a private dispute in which one party asks the court for a remedy: money, possession, a declaration of rights, or an order to do or stop doing something. This guide explains how civil litigation works in India, from choosing the right court to enforcing a decree.

## The structure of the civil courts

Civil disputes move through a hierarchy of courts, and knowing where your matter belongs is the first practical question.

- **Courts of first instance:** Most suits begin before the Civil Judge (Junior Division) or Civil Judge (Senior Division), with the dividing line usually set by the value of the claim. High-value commercial disputes may go before a Commercial Court.
- **District Court:** Hears first appeals and higher-value matters, and supervises the civil courts in the district.
- **High Court:** Hears appeals, exercises writ and supervisory jurisdiction, and in some states (such as the Bombay High Court) has original jurisdiction over high-value suits.
- **Supreme Court of India:** The final appellate court, which hears appeals by special leave and decides questions of national importance.

The governing procedural law is the **Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (CPC)**, supported by each High Court's rules.

## What kinds of disputes are "civil"

Civil litigation is broad. Common categories include:

- **Money recovery** — unpaid invoices, loans and dues (see [Money Recovery Suits](/insights/money-recovery-suit-india) and the faster [Summary Suit under Order XXXVII](/insights/summary-suit-order-37)).
- **Contract disputes** — breach, damages and specific performance.
- **Property and title** — declaration of ownership, partition and possession.
- **Injunctions** — orders to preserve the status quo (see [Injunctions and Stay Orders](/insights/injunction-stay-order-india)).
- **Family and succession** — though many of these go before dedicated Family Courts.

## How a civil suit proceeds

A civil suit follows a recognisable sequence. The pace depends on the court's docket, but the stages are consistent.

1. **Plaint and filing.** The claimant (plaintiff) files a *plaint* setting out the facts, the cause of action and the relief sought, with the required court fee and supporting documents.
2. **Notice and written statement.** The court issues summons; the defendant files a *written statement* responding to each claim, usually within 30 days (extendable, with a cap in commercial matters).
3. **Framing of issues.** The court identifies the disputed questions of fact and law that it must decide.
4. **Evidence.** Each side leads evidence — affidavits of examination-in-chief, documents, and cross-examination of witnesses.
5. **Arguments.** Counsel make final submissions on the issues.
6. **Judgment and decree.** The court delivers judgment and draws up a *decree*, which is the enforceable expression of the result.
7. **Execution.** If the losing party does not comply, the winner files execution proceedings to recover money or possession.

## Interim relief: protecting your position during the case

Litigation takes time, and a party often needs protection while the suit is pending. Courts can grant **interim relief** — most commonly a temporary injunction to preserve the status quo, attachment before judgment, or appointment of a receiver. To obtain an injunction, a party generally must show a *prima facie* case, that the balance of convenience favours them, and that they would suffer irreparable harm without the order. Our guide on [Injunctions and Stay Orders](/insights/injunction-stay-order-india) explains this in detail.

## Limitation: don't lose the right to sue

The **Limitation Act, 1963** prescribes time limits within which a suit must be filed — for example, three years for most money and contract claims, calculated from when the cause of action arose. Missing the limitation period can defeat an otherwise strong case, so assessing limitation early is essential.

## Appeals and review

A party dissatisfied with a judgment has options:

- **First appeal** on facts and law to the higher court.
- **Second appeal** to the High Court, usually limited to substantial questions of law.
- **Revision and review** in defined circumstances.
- **Special Leave Petition** to the Supreme Court under Article 136.

Each has its own limitation period, so prompt advice matters.

## Alternatives to a full trial

Court is not the only route, and often not the fastest. Depending on the contract and the dispute, parties may use:

- **Arbitration**, where there is an arbitration clause (see our [arbitration practice](/practices/arbitration)).
- **Mediation and conciliation**, now strengthened by the Mediation Act, 2023.
- **Lok Adalats**, for settlement of compoundable and money matters.

Choosing the right forum at the outset can save years. Our guide on [Arbitration vs Litigation](/insights/arbitration-vs-litigation) compares the trade-offs.

## Realistic timelines and costs

Civil litigation in India rewards patience and preparation. Timelines depend heavily on the court, the number of witnesses and interlocutory applications, and the other side's conduct. Costs include court fees (often a percentage of the claim value), professional fees and incidental expenses. A candid early assessment of merits, limitation, forum and likely timeline is the most useful thing a litigant can have.

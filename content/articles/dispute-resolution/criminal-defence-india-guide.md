Facing a criminal case in India, whether as an accused, a complainant, or a witness, is a disorienting experience. The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS), which replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, has modernised several procedural aspects while preserving the fundamental architecture of criminal defence. Understanding that architecture, from the FIR through to trial, is essential for anyone navigating the process.

## Bailable and Non-Bailable Offences

The first question any defence lawyer addresses is the classification of the offence. Schedule I of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS) classifies offences as bailable or non-bailable. In bailable offences, the accused has a right to bail: the arresting officer or the magistrate is obliged to release the person on furnishing a surety bond or a personal bond. Examples include minor road accidents, public nuisance, and certain property offences below specified thresholds.

Non-bailable offences are more serious, and bail is not automatic. The accused must apply to a magistrate or sessions court, and the court weighs factors such as the gravity of the accusation, the likelihood of the accused fleeing, the risk of tampering with evidence, and the antecedents of the accused. Section 480 BNSS lists circumstances in which bail must generally not be granted in non-bailable offences, including cases involving serious repeat offenders or offences punishable with death or imprisonment for life.

## Responding to an FIR

An FIR, or First Information Report, is the document that sets the criminal process in motion. Once a cognisable offence is reported, the police are duty-bound to register an FIR under Section 173 BNSS. A person who learns that an FIR has been registered against them should act quickly on several fronts.

First, obtain a copy of the FIR. The complainant and the accused are both entitled to a free copy. Reading the FIR carefully reveals which sections have been invoked, whether the offences are bailable or non-bailable, and the factual allegations that must be engaged. Second, assess the immediate risk of arrest. If arrest appears imminent and the offences are non-bailable, an application for anticipatory bail under Section 482 BNSS before the sessions court or the High Court is usually the first step a defence lawyer will recommend.

## Station Bail and Judicial Bail

Where the offence is bailable, an accused who appears at the police station or is arrested there can seek station bail directly from the officer in charge. This is the simplest and fastest form of release, and the officer must release the person on execution of a personal bond, with or without sureties.

For non-bailable offences, judicial bail under Section 483 BNSS is required. The application is filed before the jurisdictional magistrate or, for more serious offences, the sessions court. The defence must address the triple test: the accused will not abscond, will not tamper with evidence, and does not pose a threat to witnesses or the community. Conditions such as surrendering a passport, periodic reporting to the police station, and restraint from contacting the complainant are commonly imposed.

## Anticipatory Bail

Anticipatory bail under Section 482 BNSS is a protective order that prevents arrest. It is not a bail in the sense of releasing a person already in custody. Instead, it is a direction to the police that if the applicant is arrested for the specified offence, they shall be released on bail. The sessions court and the High Court both have concurrent jurisdiction to grant anticipatory bail.

The landmark position from Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia v. State of Punjab continues to hold that anticipatory bail should not be granted mechanically, but that courts should not be unduly restrictive either. The Supreme Court in Sushila Aggarwal v. State (NCT of Delhi) confirmed that anticipatory bail need not be limited to a fixed time and can endure until the conclusion of trial, absent specific reasons for curtailment.

## Chargesheet and Cognisance

After investigation, if the police find sufficient material, they file a chargesheet under Section 193 BNSS within the statutory time limits. Failure to file within the prescribed period triggers the accused's right to default bail under Section 187 BNSS. Once the magistrate takes cognisance of the chargesheet, the accused is formally summoned or presented for the framing of charges.

Charges are framed at a hearing where the court evaluates whether a prima facie case exists. A discharge application can be filed under Section 250 BNSS if the material on record does not disclose the essential ingredients of the offence. Discharge ends the trial before examination of witnesses begins and is therefore a valuable early remedy.

## Trial Stages

The trial in a sessions court or magistrate court follows a defined sequence. Prosecution evidence comes first: the public prosecutor examines witnesses, produces documents and exhibits, and the defence cross-examines each witness. After the prosecution closes its evidence, the court records the statement of the accused under Section 351 BNSS, giving the accused an opportunity to explain incriminating circumstances without being placed under oath.

The defence then leads its own evidence, if any. Both sides present final arguments, after which the court delivers a verdict of conviction or acquittal. At the sentencing stage following a conviction, the defence can present mitigating factors including the accused's age, background, family circumstances, and the absence of a prior criminal record.

## Quashing Under Section 528 BNSS

Section 528 BNSS preserves the inherent power of the High Court to make orders necessary to prevent abuse of the process of court or to secure the ends of justice. Quashing of an FIR or criminal proceedings is one of the most frequently invoked applications under this provision. The Supreme Court in State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal laid down the categories of cases where quashing is appropriate: where the allegations do not constitute any offence even if taken at face value, where the complaint is malafide or motivated, or where a purely civil or commercial dispute has been converted into a criminal one to gain leverage.

Settlement between the parties can also ground a quashing petition, particularly in compoundable offences under Section 359 BNSS or in cases arising out of matrimonial, commercial, or neighbourhood disputes. The High Court evaluates whether continuation of the proceedings would serve any legitimate purpose given the settlement, even in non-compoundable offences, where the facts are extraordinary.

## Practical Considerations

Criminal defence in India demands early and coordinated action. Delay in securing bail, failure to respond to police notices under Section 35 BNSS, or appearing before an investigating officer without legal advice can all prejudice the accused's position. A well-prepared defence involves understanding the investigative record, identifying favourable witnesses, preserving documentary evidence, and building a factual narrative that responds to the prosecution's case directly rather than resting on bare denials.

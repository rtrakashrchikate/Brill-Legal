A dishonoured cheque is among the most litigated matters in Indian courts, and for good reason. Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 transformed what was previously a civil obligation to pay into a criminal offence with the threat of imprisonment. The provision was designed to protect the credibility of cheques as a payment instrument and to provide creditors with an effective pressure mechanism where a debtor defaults on a cheque payment. Used correctly, it is a powerful tool for recovery.

## What Makes a Cheque Bounce Case Under Section 138

Not every dishonoured cheque gives rise to a Section 138 offence. The provision contains a specific set of conditions that must all be satisfied before a criminal complaint can be filed. Understanding each element is essential, both for a payee seeking to use the remedy and for a drawer defending against it.

The cheque must have been drawn by the accused on an account maintained with a banker. The cheque must have been issued for the discharge, in whole or in part, of a legally enforceable debt or liability. Presentation to the bank must have occurred within the validity period of the cheque, which is three months from the date shown on the face of the instrument. The cheque must have been returned unpaid, whether for insufficiency of funds in the account, for exceeding the arrangement, or for any other reason indicating that it cannot be honoured out of that account.

After dishonour, the payee must send a written demand notice to the drawer within 30 days of receiving the bank's memo of return. The drawer must then fail to make payment of the cheque amount within 15 days of receiving that notice. Only after these conditions are all met does the offence under Section 138 crystallise.

## The Mandatory Demand Notice

The demand notice is not a formality. It is a legal prerequisite, and defects in the notice can defeat an otherwise sound complaint. The notice must be in writing, addressed to the drawer at their correct address, and must demand payment of the amount of the cheque. The standard practice is to send the notice by registered post with acknowledgement due, which preserves evidence of dispatch and, through the postal service record, evidence of delivery or attempted delivery.

The 15-day window for the drawer to pay runs from the date of receipt of the notice by the drawer, not from the date of sending. Where the drawer is not available and the notice is returned unserved, the law on deemed service through proper addressing and posting has been clarified over time by the courts: a properly sent notice returned unclaimed is generally treated as served.

Common errors in demand notices include addressing the notice to the wrong person, omitting details of the cheque such as the number, date, and amount, failing to specify the amount demanded, and using language that leaves the demand ambiguous. Accused persons regularly challenge the validity of notices on these technical grounds, and such challenges sometimes succeed.

## Filing the Complaint

If the drawer does not pay within 15 days of receiving the notice, the payee's right to file a criminal complaint arises. The complaint must be filed within one month from the date on which the cause of action arose. The cause of action arises on the day after the 15-day payment window expires.

Following the Supreme Court's ruling in Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod v. State of Maharashtra (2014), the complaint must be filed before the magistrate with jurisdiction over the location where the cheque was presented for payment, namely the branch of the payee's bank. Earlier practice had allowed filing at the place where the cheque was drawn or where the payee resided, but this was definitively overruled. Strict adherence to this jurisdictional rule is required: a complaint filed before a court without jurisdiction will be returned or dismissed.

## Punishment Under Section 138

A conviction under Section 138 carries imprisonment for a term which may extend to two years, a fine which may extend to twice the amount of the cheque, or both. Courts commonly award a fine covering the cheque amount plus reasonable compensation to the payee. The threat of imprisonment and a criminal conviction record is a significant deterrent and is often what prompts settlement during the pendency of the complaint.

Section 138 complaints can be compounded: the parties can reach a settlement during the proceedings, and the court can record the settlement and close the matter. The Supreme Court has recognised that Section 138 is primarily intended to serve the purpose of recovery, and settlements that result in payment to the payee are generally viewed favourably.

## The Section 139 Presumption

Once the basic facts are established, Section 139 of the Negotiable Instruments Act raises a presumption in the payee's favour: the court presumes that the cheque was issued for the discharge of a debt or liability unless the accused proves otherwise. This shifts the burden to the accused at the trial stage.

The burden of rebuttal is not impossibly heavy, but it is real. A bare denial is insufficient. The accused must produce material, whether documentary or oral, that raises a reasonable doubt about whether the cheque was issued for the discharge of an existing legally enforceable debt. This presumption, combined with the strict procedural requirements on the payee, is what gives Section 138 its effectiveness as a recovery tool.

## Available Defences

Accused persons in Section 138 cases regularly raise several categories of defence. The most substantial is that the cheque was not issued for a legally enforceable debt or liability. If the cheque was given as a security deposit, as a post-dated instrument for a conditional obligation that had not yet arisen, or in circumstances where the underlying transaction was illegal, the discharge of debt element is not satisfied and no offence is made out.

Other defences commonly raised include that the cheque was signed in blank and misused by the payee, that the underlying liability had already been discharged by other means before the cheque was presented, that the demand notice was defective or was not received, that the complaint was filed out of time, and that the cheque presented was not the cheque given by the accused. Courts examine each of these carefully against the documentary record and the Section 139 presumption.

## Parallel Civil Recovery

A Section 138 complaint and a civil recovery suit can be filed simultaneously and run in parallel. They are independent remedies: the criminal complaint does not merge into the civil suit, and the civil suit does not preclude the criminal one. Running both simultaneously maximises pressure on the defaulting drawer while independently enforcing the underlying debt. Where the primary objective is recovery, the combination of criminal pressure and direct civil enforcement is a commonly used and effective strategy.

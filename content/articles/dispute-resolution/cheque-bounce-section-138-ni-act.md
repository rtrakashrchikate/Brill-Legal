A bounced cheque is one of the most litigated matters in Indian courts. Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act 1881 converted what was once merely a civil wrong into a criminal offence — not because bouncing a cheque is inherently criminal, but because the threat of prosecution is an effective pressure on drawers to honour their payment obligations. Used correctly, it is a powerful recovery tool.

## The elements of the Section 138 offence

For a Section 138 complaint to succeed, the payee must establish:

1. A cheque was drawn by the accused (drawer) on a bank account maintained with a banker
2. The cheque was issued for the discharge — in whole or part — of a debt or liability
3. The cheque was presented to the bank within its validity period (three months)
4. The cheque was returned unpaid — either for insufficiency of funds or for a reason indicating that it cannot be honoured out of the account
5. A written demand notice was sent by the payee to the drawer within 30 days of receiving the bank's memo of return
6. The drawer failed to pay within 15 days of receiving the notice

Each element matters. A cheque given as a gift, a security deposit returned without consideration, or a post-dated cheque that cannot be connected to an existing debt are areas where element (2) is contested.

## The demand notice

The demand notice is both a legal requirement and a strategic document. It must be in writing, addressed to the drawer, and demand payment of the cheque amount. It is typically sent by registered post with acknowledgement due — preserving proof of delivery and the date of service. The 15-day payment window runs from the drawer's receipt of the notice (not from the date of sending).

Common errors in the notice — wrong address, omission of the cheque details, failure to specify the amount demanded — can be exploited by the accused to challenge the complaint's maintainability.

## Filing the complaint

If the drawer does not pay within 15 days of receiving the notice, the payee may file a complaint before the competent Magistrate. The limitation period for filing is one month from the date on which the cause of action arose (the day after the 15-day payment period expires).

Following the Supreme Court's ruling in *Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod v. State of Maharashtra* (2014), the complaint must be filed at the court having jurisdiction over the location where the cheque was presented for collection — meaning the branch of the payee's bank. This is a strict rule and the court cannot take jurisdiction otherwise.

## Punishment and relief

Section 138 carries imprisonment up to two years, a fine up to twice the cheque amount, or both. Courts routinely award a fine that covers the cheque amount plus reasonable compensation to the payee. In practice, many cases settle during trial because the threat of a conviction and imprisonment record is a powerful incentive for the drawer.

The accused may raise a statutory presumption to their disadvantage: Section 139 presumes that the cheque was issued for the discharge of a debt. The accused must rebut this presumption — showing that no debt existed, that the cheque was issued as security only, or that the consideration has already been returned.

## Common defences

Defences that accused persons commonly raise include:

- The cheque was given as security, not for discharge of a debt
- The cheque was signed in blank and misused
- The liability has been discharged or settled
- The demand notice was defective or not received
- The complaint is time-barred

Courts scrutinise these defences against the Section 139 presumption. The burden of rebuttal on the accused is not onerous, but it is real — a bare denial is insufficient.

## Parallel civil recovery

Running a Section 138 complaint and a civil recovery suit simultaneously is common and effective. The criminal complaint applies pressure; the civil suit directly enforces the underlying debt. If the accused is acquitted, the civil suit remains; if the civil suit succeeds first, the criminal complaint may continue for its punitive component. A settlement in the criminal proceedings (which the Supreme Court has recognised as capable of leading to compounding) does not automatically extinguish the civil claim unless the settlement terms say so.

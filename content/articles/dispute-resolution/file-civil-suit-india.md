A civil suit is the formal legal mechanism for enforcing private rights — recovering money owed, protecting property, compelling performance of a contract, or establishing a legal status. The Code of Civil Procedure 1908 (CPC) governs the process. While the structure is consistent across courts, the experience of litigation varies widely with the court chosen and the care with which the case is prepared.

## Step one: identifying the right court

Before a plaint is drafted, two jurisdictional questions must be answered.

**Pecuniary jurisdiction** — the value of the claim — determines the level of court. Civil courts are typically divided into a Junior Division (for claims below a threshold, often one lakh rupees or a state-specific figure) and a Senior Division for larger claims. Claims above certain values go to the High Court's original side (in Bombay, Calcutta and Madras) or, for commercial disputes, to the Commercial Court under the Commercial Courts Act 2015.

**Territorial jurisdiction** — the geographic connection to the court — follows Section 20 CPC: the suit may be filed where the defendant ordinarily resides, carries on business, or personally works for gain; or where the cause of action wholly or partly arose. In contract disputes, the place of performance is also relevant. Where multiple defendants are involved, the rules allow the suit to be filed where any one of them resides, subject to leave of the court.

Getting jurisdiction wrong means the suit is returned or dismissed at the threshold — not just inconvenient but potentially fatal to a time-barred claim.

## Drafting the plaint

The plaint is the document that commences the suit. Under Order VII CPC, it must state:

- The name and address of the plaintiff and defendant
- The facts constituting the cause of action and when it arose
- The relief claimed, specifically
- The grounds of jurisdiction (pecuniary and territorial)
- A statement that the suit is within time under the Limitation Act
- A verification clause sworn by the plaintiff

Clarity and specificity matter. A vague plaint invites preliminary objections and interlocutory skirmishes that delay the case before it begins.

## Court fees and filing

Court fee must be paid at filing. In Maharashtra (and most states), the fee is calculated as a percentage of the amount claimed under the Court Fees Act 1870, as amended. For declaratory and injunction suits, the fee is assessed on the value of the relief. Courts return plaints where the fee is insufficient.

The plaint and accompanying documents (cause of action papers, annexures, vakalatnama authorising counsel) are lodged at the filing counter of the relevant court. The registry scrutinises the filing and, if in order, the suit is numbered and assigned.

## Service of summons

After filing, the court issues summons to the defendant directing appearance on a specified date. Service is generally by registered post, bailiff, or (for commercial suits) by electronic means. Proper service is jurisdictional — a decree without valid service on the defendant can be challenged. Where the defendant evades service, substituted service (by affixture, advertisement, or electronic means) can be sought.

## The defendant's written statement

Once served, the defendant has a fixed period to file a written statement. Under the CPC (and strictly enforced in commercial suits under the 2015 Act), the written statement must be filed within 30 days, extendable by the court to a maximum of 90 days in ordinary suits and 120 days in commercial suits — after which the right to file is forfeited. The written statement admits, denies, and explains; it also raises any counter-claim or set-off, and any preliminary objections (limitation, jurisdiction, maintainability).

## Interim relief at the outset

One of the most important steps in many suits is an application for interim injunction filed alongside or shortly after the plaint. The court may, on application, restrain the defendant from creating third-party rights in disputed property, transferring assets, continuing a breach, or doing any act that would defeat the final relief sought. The threshold is the well-established three-part test of prima facie case, balance of convenience, and irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted.

For urgent matters — where the defendant is about to act immediately — an ex-parte ad-interim injunction can be sought, which the court grants without hearing the other side and then re-examines after the defendant has appeared.

## Trial and evidence

After pleadings close, issues are framed — the specific points in dispute that the court must resolve. Evidence is led on those issues, first by the plaintiff, then the defendant. Documentary evidence is introduced through witness affidavits (examination-in-chief) and tested in cross-examination.

Commercial courts have a specific case-management timetable. For ordinary civil suits, timelines depend heavily on the individual court's docket.

## Judgment, decree, and execution

Once arguments are concluded, the court delivers a judgment, from which a formal decree is drawn. The decree is the court's operative order. If the defendant does not comply voluntarily, the decree-holder may file an execution petition seeking the court's machinery to enforce it — through attachment and sale of assets, arrest, or appointment of a court receiver.

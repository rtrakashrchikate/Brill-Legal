A civil suit is the formal legal mechanism for enforcing private rights, including recovering money owed, protecting property, compelling performance of a contract, and establishing a legal status. The Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (CPC) governs the process from start to finish. While the structure is consistent across courts, the practical experience of litigation varies significantly with the court chosen and the care with which the case is prepared from the outset.

## Identifying the Right Court

Before a plaint is drafted, two jurisdictional questions must be answered. Getting either wrong means the suit is returned or dismissed at the threshold, which can be fatal to a claim that is approaching its limitation deadline.

### Pecuniary Jurisdiction

Pecuniary jurisdiction refers to the monetary value of the claim and determines the level of court. Civil courts are typically divided by the value of the dispute. In Maharashtra, the Civil Judge (Junior Division) hears cases up to a specified value threshold, while the Civil Judge (Senior Division) handles larger claims. Commercial disputes above the specified value threshold under the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 must be filed before the designated Commercial Court or the Commercial Division of the High Court. The Bombay High Court's original side has jurisdiction over high-value claims with the requisite territorial connection to Mumbai.

### Territorial Jurisdiction

Territorial jurisdiction is governed by Section 20 CPC. A suit may be filed where the defendant ordinarily resides, carries on business, or personally works for gain, or where the cause of action wholly or partly arose. In contract disputes, the place of performance and the place where the contract was made are also relevant connecting factors. Where there are multiple defendants, the suit may be filed where any one of them resides, subject to leave of the court for the others.

## Drafting the Plaint

The plaint is the document that commences the suit. Under Order VII CPC, it must contain the name and address of the plaintiff and every defendant, the facts constituting the cause of action and when it arose, a precise description of the relief claimed, the grounds on which the court has pecuniary and territorial jurisdiction, a statement that the suit is filed within the period prescribed by the Limitation Act, and a verification clause sworn or affirmed by the plaintiff.

Clarity in the plaint is not merely a formality. A vague or poorly drafted plaint invites preliminary objections from the defendant and interlocutory skirmishes that delay the substantive hearing before it even begins. Documents relied upon in the plaint should be annexed at the time of filing.

## Court Fee and Filing

Court fee must be paid at the time of filing under the applicable state Court Fees Act. In Maharashtra, the fee is a percentage of the amount claimed or the value of the relief sought. For declaratory suits and suits seeking injunctions where no money is claimed, the court fee is calculated on the value of the subject matter as assessed by the plaintiff. Courts return plaints where the fee is insufficient, requiring the plaintiff to make up the deficiency before the suit is numbered.

The plaint, accompanying documents, and the vakalatnama authorising counsel are lodged at the filing counter of the relevant court. The registry scrutinises the filing for formal compliance and, if in order, assigns a suit number and schedules the first date.

## Service of Summons

After a suit is filed and numbered, the court issues summons to the defendant directing appearance on a specified date. Service may be effected by registered post with acknowledgement due, by a court bailiff, or, in commercial matters, by electronic means. Proper service is a jurisdictional requirement: a decree passed without valid service on the defendant can be challenged in execution or appeal.

Where a defendant evades service or cannot be located, substituted service is available. The court can direct service by affixture at the defendant's last known address, by advertisement in a newspaper, or by electronic means. The plaintiff must demonstrate the steps taken to effect ordinary service before substituted service will be ordered.

## The Written Statement

Once served, the defendant has a prescribed period to file a written statement responding to the plaint. Under the CPC, the written statement must be filed within 30 days, extendable by the court to a maximum of 90 days in ordinary civil suits. Under the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, the maximum period is 120 days, after which the right to file is forfeited and the court must proceed without the defendant's written statement.

The written statement admits what is accurate, denies what is not, and explains the defendant's version of events. It must also raise any preliminary objections such as limitation, lack of jurisdiction, and non-maintainability, as well as any counter-claim or set-off the defendant wishes to pursue against the plaintiff.

## Interim Relief

One of the most strategically important steps in many suits is an application for interim injunction, filed alongside or shortly after the plaint. The court may, on application, restrain the defendant from creating third-party rights in disputed property, alienating assets, continuing a breach of contract, or taking any action that would defeat the final relief sought. The standard is the well-established three-part test: prima facie case, balance of convenience, and irreparable harm if the injunction is refused.

Where urgency makes it necessary to act before the defendant can be served and heard, an ex-parte ad-interim injunction can be sought. The court grants this without hearing the other side but then re-examines it after the defendant appears. Obtaining an ex-parte order requires full and frank disclosure of all material facts, and suppression of adverse material is grounds for setting aside the order.

## Issues, Evidence, and Trial

After pleadings close, the court frames issues: the specific questions of fact and law in dispute that must be resolved by the judgment. Evidence is then led on those issues. The plaintiff goes first, presenting documentary evidence through affidavits of examination-in-chief, which the defendant may challenge in cross-examination. After the plaintiff closes evidence, the defendant leads their own.

Commercial Courts have strict case-management timetables designed to prevent indefinite adjournments. Ordinary civil courts are more variable in timeline, depending on the docket of the individual court and the conduct of the parties.

## Decree and Execution

Once arguments are completed, the court delivers a judgment setting out its findings. From the judgment, a formal decree is drawn up, which is the operative order of the court. If the defendant does not comply voluntarily, the decree-holder may file an execution petition seeking the court's machinery to enforce it. Execution remedies include attachment and sale of the judgment-debtor's moveable and immoveable property, delivery of possession, and in limited circumstances the arrest of the judgment-debtor. The execution court can also examine the judgment-debtor about their assets and compel disclosure.

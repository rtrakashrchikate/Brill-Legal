The Consumer Protection Act 2019 replaced a law that had governed consumer disputes since 1986. The replacement was not cosmetic — it addressed genuine gaps that had opened up as e-commerce grew and product liability litigation became routine in other jurisdictions. This guide explains the changes that matter most for consumers and what they mean in practice.

## Why the 1986 Act was replaced

The Consumer Protection Act 1986 was drafted for a pre-internet economy. It did not mention e-commerce, imposed no product liability rules on manufacturers independent of contract, set pecuniary limits that had become inadequate for high-value disputes, and provided no fast-track mechanism for straightforward cases. By the 2010s, the courts were handling complaints about online fraud, defective consumer electronics, and medical negligence under a framework designed for a different era. The 2019 Act addressed each of these points.

## Who is a "consumer" under the 2019 Act

The definition is wide: a person who buys goods or hires services for consideration, and includes any beneficiary of those goods or services. Crucially, a person who buys goods for resale or for commercial purposes is excluded — the Act protects end-consumers, not businesses purchasing inputs.

The 2019 Act added **e-commerce** explicitly. A consumer who buys through an online marketplace, a subscription platform, or a direct-to-consumer website is a consumer within the Act. This matters because disputes with platforms — delivery failures, counterfeit goods, subscription billing — are now squarely within the consumer forum's jurisdiction.

## The three-tier forum and revised limits

The forum structure remains in three tiers, but the 2019 Act significantly raised the pecuniary limits:

### District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission
Handles complaints where the value of goods or services paid — not the compensation claimed — does not exceed one crore rupees. Most individual consumer disputes fall here.

### State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission
Handles complaints between one crore and ten crore rupees, and appeals from the District Commission.

### National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission
Handles complaints exceeding ten crore rupees, and appeals from the State Commission. Revision petitions from the National Commission lie to the Supreme Court.

Jurisdiction is determined by the value of the goods or services paid, not the quantum of compensation claimed. This is a source of confusion — a consumer who paid one lakh rupees for a refrigerator but claims ten lakh in compensation files before the District Commission.

## Product liability — a genuinely new chapter

Chapter VI introduced **product liability** as a standalone concept in Indian consumer law. A manufacturer, service provider, or product seller may be held liable for harm caused by a defective product or a deficient service, even without a contractual relationship with the consumer who suffered the harm.

The Act defines a "product liability action" as a claim for compensation from injury or harm caused by a defective product or service. Defectiveness includes manufacturing defects, design defects, and failures to warn adequately of known risks. A consumer who is injured by a faulty appliance, a contaminated food product, or a medical device that malfunctions has a cause of action directly against the manufacturer — not merely a claim in contract against the seller.

This aligns Indian law more closely with jurisdictions where product liability is a developed tort, and the implications for manufacturers, importers, and sellers of consumer goods are significant.

## The Central Consumer Protection Authority

The 2019 Act created the **Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA)**, a regulatory body with powers that the old Act lacked. The CCPA can:

- investigate unfair trade practices and misleading advertisements suo motu
- issue orders to recall products, refund prices, and discontinue practices
- impose penalties on manufacturers and endorsers for misleading advertisements
- intervene in class-action situations where a large number of consumers are affected

The CCPA functions separately from the consumer forums. A consumer can report a matter to the CCPA for regulatory action while simultaneously — or alternatively — filing a complaint before the forum for personal compensation.

## E-commerce obligations

The Act and rules made under it impose specific duties on e-commerce entities: displaying seller information, not imposing unfair conditions, ensuring a grievance redressal mechanism, and complying with return and refund policies stated at the time of purchase. Platforms that list third-party sellers carry responsibilities for the accuracy of the information displayed and, in some circumstances, for the products sold through them.

## Mediation as a first step

When a complaint is admitted, the forum may, with the parties' consent, refer the matter to a Consumer Mediation Cell. The process is structured: a trained mediator facilitates a settlement conversation, and if it succeeds, the terms are recorded and placed before the forum for confirmation as an order. If it fails, the complaint returns to the forum for adjudication. Mediation adds a potential fast-track for cases where the consumer's primary interest is practical resolution rather than precedent.

## What this means for filing a complaint

For a consumer pursuing a complaint today, the 2019 Act means:

- **Jurisdiction is clear** for e-commerce disputes — the platform and the seller are both within reach
- **Product liability** provides a direct claim against manufacturers for physical harm from defective products
- **Higher pecuniary limits** mean most individual complaints sit comfortably within the District Commission's jurisdiction
- **Online filing** through the e-Daakhil portal (mandated under the Act) removes the need to appear in person for filing
- **Mediation** may offer a faster resolution path for straightforward cases before the full hearing process begins

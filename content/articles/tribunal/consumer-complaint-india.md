The Consumer Protection Act 2019, which replaced the Consumer Protection Act 1986, is the primary legislation protecting buyers of goods and services in India from unfair trade practices, defective products, and deficient services. The 2019 legislation introduced significant improvements — higher monetary jurisdictions for each forum tier, coverage of e-commerce transactions and direct sellers, a new Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) with suo motu powers, and a mandatory mediation step for eligible disputes. For consumers dealing with builders, e-commerce platforms, telecom providers, airlines, insurance companies, or any other service provider, understanding how the consumer complaint system works is the first step toward effective redress.

## Who Is a Consumer?

Under Section 2(7) of the Consumer Protection Act 2019, a consumer is a person who:

- Buys goods for a consideration — whether fully or partly paid, or under a deferred payment arrangement — but does not include a person who buys goods for resale or for commercial purposes; or
- Hires or avails of any service for a consideration, but does not include a person who avails such service for any commercial purpose

The exclusion for commercial purposes has been interpreted by courts on a case-by-case basis — the test is whether the purchase is for personal use of the buyer or primarily for profit-generating business activity. The 2019 Act expressly included electronic transactions in the definition, covering purchases on e-commerce platforms. A person who receives goods as a beneficiary of a transaction (even if they did not personally pay) can also qualify as a consumer in appropriate circumstances, such as a passenger on a ticket purchased by another person.

## What Constitutes a Deficiency in Service or Defect in Goods

**Deficiency in service** is defined broadly under the Act as any fault, imperfection, shortcoming, or inadequacy in the quality, nature, and manner of performance that is required to be maintained by law or has been undertaken to be performed under a contract. This covers delayed delivery, poor workmanship, failure to provide promised facilities, negligent professional services, and breach of warranty, among many other situations.

**Defective goods** refers to goods that do not conform to quality standards imposed by law (such as Bureau of Indian Standards requirements), or that have a manufacturing defect, design defect, or are not fit for the purpose for which they were represented as suitable.

The 2019 Act also introduced a product liability chapter, which allows consumers to bring claims against manufacturers, sellers, and service providers for physical harm or death caused by defective products — going beyond the traditional "deficiency in service" framework.

## Forum Selection: Choosing the Right Commission

The 2019 Act revised the monetary jurisdiction thresholds significantly compared to the 1986 law:

- **District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission (DCDRC):** Claims up to ₹1 crore. In Pune, the DCDRC is at the district headquarters.
- **State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission (SCDRC):** Claims above ₹1 crore and up to ₹10 crore. In Maharashtra, the SCDRC is in Mumbai.
- **National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission (NCDRC):** Claims above ₹10 crore. Located in New Delhi.

Jurisdiction is determined by the total value of the goods or services and the compensation claimed together — not just the purchase price. If you paid ₹50 lakh for a flat and are claiming ₹80 lakh in total (purchase price plus damages), the aggregate claim value determines jurisdiction.

In addition to the value criterion, the complaint may be filed where the complainant resides or personally works for gain, where the opposite party carries on business, or where the cause of action arose — giving consumers meaningful flexibility in choosing a convenient forum.

## The e-Daakhil Process: Filing Online

The e-Daakhil portal (edaakhil.nic.in) allows consumers across India to file complaints online with any of the three tiers of consumer commissions. The process involves:

1. **Registration** on the portal using a mobile number and email address
2. **Drafting the complaint** — the portal provides structured fields for complainant and opposite party details, the facts of the case, and the relief claimed
3. **Uploading supporting documents** — purchase receipts, agreements, warranty cards, correspondence with the service provider, and any expert reports or photographs
4. **Paying the filing fee** — which varies by claim amount and is paid electronically through the portal
5. **Submission** — the commission issues a notice to the opposite party and schedules the first hearing

Physical filing at the commission's office remains available, but e-Daakhil has substantially improved access — particularly for complainants who do not live near commission offices or who find court visits burdensome.

## Reliefs Available

A consumer commission can grant a wide range of reliefs under the 2019 Act, including:

- **Replacement** of defective goods with goods of the same description free of charge
- **Repair** of defective goods
- **Refund** of the price paid for defective goods or inadequate services
- **Compensation** for any loss or injury suffered — including compensation for mental agony, harassment, and financial loss flowing from the deficiency
- **Removal of the deficiency** in service — a direction to provide the service that was contracted for
- **Discontinuance of unfair trade practices** and a direction not to repeat them
- **Punitive damages** in appropriate cases where the deficiency was deliberate or caused widespread harm

## Time Limits

A consumer complaint must be filed within two years from the date on which the cause of action arose. The commission has the power to condone delay if the complainant demonstrates sufficient cause — but condonation is not guaranteed, and the later a complaint is filed, the harder the threshold of sufficient cause becomes to meet. Consumers should file as soon as possible once the nature of the deficiency is clear and attempts at direct resolution have been made and failed.

## Common Complaint Categories in Practice

**Real estate:** Delayed possession by builders, failure to deliver promised amenities, construction defects, and mismatch between contracted and delivered carpet area. Consumer forums retain jurisdiction for many real estate complaints alongside MahaRERA — though complainants should be cautious about pursuing both forums simultaneously for the same cause of action, as courts have raised questions about forum shopping in this context.

**E-commerce:** Defective products, products different from those described online, non-refund of returned goods, delivery failures, and misleading product specifications from major platforms and marketplaces.

**Telecom:** Billing errors, unresolved service degradation complaints, wrongful disconnection, and failure to honour advertised tariff plans.

**Insurance:** Wrongful repudiation of claims, delayed settlement, excessive deductions, and mis-selling of policies — particularly health and motor insurance.

**Banking and financial services:** Mis-selling of investment products, undisclosed charges, failures in loan processing, credit card dispute handling, and ATM transaction failures.

## Mediation Under the 2019 Act

The Consumer Protection Act 2019 introduced a statutory mediation framework. Under Section 37, where the commission believes a dispute may be settled by mediation and both parties consent, the matter is referred to the Consumer Mediation Cell attached to the commission. The process is confidential, informal, and time-bound. If a settlement is reached, it is recorded and passed as an order of the commission. If mediation fails, the complaint returns to adjudication without any prejudice from the mediation process.

Mediation is particularly useful where the parties have an ongoing relationship, where the amounts at stake are modest relative to the cost and time of litigation, or where the primary goal is a refund or replacement rather than a contested hearing.

The consumer complaint system in India has become significantly more accessible since the 2019 Act reforms. The e-Daakhil facility, revised jurisdictional thresholds, and the integrated mediation framework collectively offer consumers a practical path to redress that does not always require professional representation — though legal advice is valuable in larger, more complex, or vigorously contested matters.

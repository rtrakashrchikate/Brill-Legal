The Consumer Protection Act 2019 represents a significant modernisation of India's consumer rights framework, replacing the 1986 Act after more than three decades during which the economy, the marketplace, and the nature of consumer transactions changed beyond recognition. The 2019 Act introduced several important improvements: the e-Daakhil online filing portal, which allows consumers to file complaints from anywhere in India without attending the commission's office in person; enhanced pecuniary jurisdiction thresholds that brought large-value complaints within the reach of district and state commissions; provisions for product liability that impose direct obligations on manufacturers and service providers; and a strengthened mediation framework that creates structured opportunities for settlement before adversarial proceedings become entrenched. For individuals who have suffered harm from defective goods, deficient services, or unfair trade practices, the 2019 Act provides a more accessible and more powerful set of remedies than its predecessor.

## Who Is a Consumer?

The definition of "consumer" under Section 2(7) of the 2019 Act is the starting point for any complaint. A consumer is a person who buys any goods for consideration, or avails any service for consideration, provided the goods were not purchased or the services availed for the purpose of resale or for any commercial purpose. The exclusion of commercial purchasers is important: a company that buys office equipment in the course of its business is generally not a "consumer" for purposes of the Act, since the transaction is commercial in nature.

However, there is an important carve-out for self-employment: a person who buys goods or avails services exclusively for the purpose of earning a livelihood by means of self-employment — a driver who purchases a vehicle to operate as a taxi, a tailor who purchases a sewing machine for their small workshop — is treated as a consumer even though the goods or services are used for an income-generating purpose. This carve-out has been generously interpreted by the courts and consumer commissions to include many small operators whose commercial activity is incidental to their livelihood. The definition also encompasses "services" broadly — including banking, insurance, transport, telecommunications, housing construction, medical treatment, and educational services — meaning that the Act's protections extend well beyond the retail purchase of goods.

## What Wrongs Can You Complain About

The 2019 Act recognises several categories of wrong that ground a consumer complaint. Deficiency in service — defined as any fault, imperfection, shortcoming, or inadequacy in the quality, nature, or manner of performance of a service that is required to be maintained by or under any law, or that has been undertaken to be performed by a person pursuant to a contract — is the most commonly invoked ground. A builder who fails to deliver a flat on time, a bank that mishandles a transaction, a hospital that provides substandard care, or an airline that fails to perform the booked service all potentially commit deficiency in service.

Defective goods — goods that are unsafe or that do not conform to the quality, quantity, potency, purity, or standard required by law or the seller's own representation — ground a separate category of complaint. The 2019 Act also introduced product liability under Chapter VI, which allows a consumer to claim compensation directly from a product manufacturer, product service provider, or product seller for harm caused by a defective product, without needing to establish traditional contractual or tortious grounds. Unfair trade practices — including misleading advertisements, deceptive pricing, and false claims about goods or services — and restrictive trade practices that prevent competition or deny consumers access to goods or services are also actionable under the Act.

## Forum Selection: Which Commission to Approach

The consumer complaints machinery in India is organised on a three-tier basis by pecuniary value, and selecting the correct forum is essential — a complaint filed before the wrong commission will be returned for refiling. The District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission handles complaints where the value of goods or services and the compensation claimed does not exceed ₹50 lakhs. The State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission handles complaints where the value and compensation sought exceeds ₹50 lakhs but does not exceed ₹2 crores. The National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission (NCDRC) in New Delhi handles complaints where the value and compensation exceeds ₹2 crores.

These thresholds were revised upward by the 2019 Act from the significantly lower figures under the 1986 Act, and they should be verified against any subsequent notification because the government has power to revise them. Geographic jurisdiction for a District Commission is determined by the location where the complainant resides, works for gain, or carries on business — or where the opposite party has its office or the cause of action arose. The 2019 Act's jurisdictional provisions are more flexible than the 1986 Act's, allowing consumers to file in the location most convenient to them.

## Filing the Complaint: Step by Step

The e-Daakhil portal at edaakhil.nic.in is the primary filing mechanism under the 2019 Act. The complainant registers on the portal with a valid email address and mobile number, and creates a complaint by selecting the appropriate commission, filling in the complaint details (the parties, the transaction, the nature of the deficiency, and the reliefs sought), and uploading supporting documents. Essential documents include proof of the purchase or service transaction — a sale invoice, a service agreement, a booking confirmation — all written correspondence with the opposite party, any receipts or evidence of payment, evidence of the deficiency (photographs, inspection reports, medical records as applicable), and any prior complaint or response from the opposite party. Filing fees are paid online through the portal.

The complaint itself must clearly state the facts constituting the deficiency or defect, the relief claimed, and the legal basis — whether deficiency in service, defective goods, product liability, or unfair trade practice. A well-drafted complaint is concise but complete: it tells the full story, exhibits all key documents, and states the specific reliefs sought with clarity.

## Reliefs Available

The range of reliefs available under the 2019 Act is broader than many complainants realise. The commission may direct the repair of defective goods, replacement with goods of similar description, or a full refund of the purchase price. For deficiency in service, it may direct the service provider to rectify the deficiency, redo the service, or refund the charges paid. The commission may award compensation to the consumer for any loss or injury suffered due to the negligence of the opposite party — including compensation for mental agony and harassment, which the courts have interpreted broadly. Interest on the amounts refunded may also be awarded.

In cases involving unfair or restrictive trade practices, the commission may additionally direct discontinuation of the practice, issue corrective advertisement orders, or impose punitive damages. For product liability claims under Chapter VI, the commission may award compensation for death, personal injury, property damage, or economic loss caused by a defective product.

## Mediation Under the 2019 Act

One of the significant procedural innovations of the 2019 Act is the mandatory reference to mediation at the first hearing. Where the commission is satisfied that there is an element of settlement in the complaint, it shall direct the parties to opt for mediation before proceeding further. Consumer mediation cells have been established at each District, State, and National Commission. The mediation process is time-bound — the parties attempt to reach a settlement within a specified period — and if a settlement is reached, it is reduced to writing, signed by the parties, and placed before the commission, which then passes an order in terms of the settlement. A mediated settlement under the 2019 Act is as final and binding as a commission order. Where mediation fails, the complaint is returned to the commission for regular hearing. Mediation is worth approaching constructively — it resolves disputes faster and with less acrimony than contested proceedings, and the mediator's confidentiality obligation means that statements made in mediation cannot be used in the hearing.

## Common Complaint Types

Real estate and housing disputes — particularly involving builder delay, construction defects, and misrepresentation of specifications — are among the most common complaints before consumer commissions. The Supreme Court has consistently affirmed that buying a flat from a builder is a consumer transaction, and builders' failure to deliver possession on time or deliver promised amenities constitutes deficiency in service. Where the project is also registered with MahaRERA, the consumer may choose to file before either the RERA authority or the consumer commission, depending on the nature of the relief sought — RERA is generally faster and more specialised for possession and interest claims, while the consumer commission may offer broader compensation reliefs.

E-commerce complaints have grown significantly in volume as online retail has expanded. The 2019 Act specifically addresses e-commerce platforms, treating them as service providers and imposing obligations of disclosure, product authenticity, and grievance redressal. Insurance claim repudiation is another high-volume category — insurers who repudiate claims on technical or narrow grounds that are inconsistent with the policyholder's reasonable expectations face consumer complaints with increasing regularity, and the commissions have developed a substantial body of precedent on the standards applicable to repudiation. Telecom deficiency complaints — dropped calls, billing errors, unreasonable service interruptions — also constitute a significant share of the consumer commission's workload.

## Time Limit and Limitation

A consumer complaint must be filed within two years from the date on which the cause of action arose. The cause of action arises when the deficiency or defect is discovered or ought reasonably to have been discovered — not necessarily on the date of purchase. In cases of continuing deficiency — a builder who continues to delay possession month after month — each month of continued delay is a fresh cause of action, and the two-year period runs from the last act of deficiency. The commission may condone delay beyond two years if the complainant satisfies it that there was sufficient cause for the delay — though the burden of explanation lies on the complainant, and unexplained long delays are difficult to excuse.

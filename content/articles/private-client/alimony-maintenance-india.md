Maintenance is the obligation one spouse bears to financially support the other when the marriage breaks down and the parties are no longer living together. The obligation can arise under multiple statutes simultaneously, through different courts and with different standards of assessment. Understanding which statutory route applies, how courts quantify maintenance, and how orders are enforced is essential for anyone navigating a matrimonial dispute in India.

## The Statutory Framework

### Section 24 HMA: Interim Maintenance During Proceedings

Section 24 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 provides for maintenance pendente lite: maintenance payable while matrimonial proceedings are ongoing. Either spouse may apply, and the provision applies equally regardless of whether the applicant is the petitioner or the respondent in the main proceedings. The court looks at the applicant's income and the respondent's income and makes an order for the pendency period.

Section 24 also provides for payment of litigation expenses, recognising that a financially dependent spouse should not be unable to participate effectively in proceedings because the other spouse controls the family finances. Applications under Section 24 are dealt with relatively quickly, typically within the first few hearings of the main proceeding, because their purpose is to maintain a level playing field during what may be a prolonged case.

### Section 25 HMA: Permanent Alimony and Maintenance

Section 25 empowers the court to award permanent alimony and maintenance at the time of passing a decree in any proceedings under the Hindu Marriage Act, or at any time subsequently on application. The order may direct payment in a lump sum or as periodic payments, and may include a charge on the respondent's property to secure the obligation.

The court's discretion under Section 25 is wide. The relevant factors include the respondent's own income and other property, the income and property of the applicant, the conduct of the parties, and all other circumstances of the case. Permanent alimony is not automatic and is not simply a percentage of the respondent's income: the court exercises judgment about what is fair and adequate in the specific circumstances of the marriage.

Courts increasingly favour lump-sum settlements over periodic maintenance, and for good reason. A lump sum provides finality and avoids the complications of future modification proceedings, disputes about payment, and the need to police compliance over years. Where the respondent has identifiable assets, a lump sum that transfers or charges those assets can be more practical than a stream of monthly payments.

### Section 144 BNSS: Maintenance Before a Magistrate

Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS), which replaced Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, provides a separate and parallel maintenance route before a Magistrate. This provision applies across all religions and communities, irrespective of personal law. A wife, minor children, and certain other dependants may claim maintenance where they are unable to maintain themselves and the person obligated to maintain them has sufficient means.

The Section 144 BNSS route has practical advantages in certain situations. Magistrates can move to an interim order relatively quickly compared to Family Court timelines. The enforcement mechanism, including civil imprisonment for default, provides direct pressure. However, the quantum awarded tends to be more modest than Family Court awards, and there is a statutory cap on the rate at which interest on arrears accumulates. Many claimants pursue Section 144 BNSS proceedings alongside matrimonial proceedings in the Family Court, using the Magistrate's order for immediate financial relief while the Family Court considers a larger permanent award.

### The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005

The Domestic Violence Act provides for monetary relief orders as part of the broader protection available to a woman making a domestic violence complaint. The Magistrate can award maintenance covering living expenses, medical expenses, and losses arising from the respondent's conduct, without waiting for divorce or separation proceedings to conclude. Monetary relief under the DV Act operates alongside and not in substitution of other maintenance routes, and the amounts awarded can be considered by courts in related Family Court proceedings.

## Quantifying Maintenance: What Courts Assess

There is no statutory formula for maintenance quantum in India. Courts exercise discretion based on a weighing of several factors, and outcomes can vary significantly depending on the court and the individual judge.

The needs of the claimant are assessed against their actual living expenses, housing situation, medical requirements, and, where applicable, the needs of children in their care. The means of the respondent are examined including salary slips, income tax returns, bank statements, business accounts, and any evidence of undisclosed income. Courts are alert to suppression of income and are willing to draw inferences from lifestyle and expenditure patterns where declared income appears implausibly low.

The standard of living maintained during the marriage is an important anchor in this assessment. Courts approach maintenance as an attempt to allow the claimant to continue at a broadly comparable standard, adjusted for the reality that the household has now separated into two. A long marriage during which the claimant was out of the workforce is treated very differently from a short marriage between two employed professionals.

The claimant's own earning capacity and independent resources are also considered. Maintenance is not designed as indefinite full support for an able-bodied adult who has reasonable prospects of self-sufficiency. Courts reduce or decline maintenance where the claimant has substantial independent income or assets, or where their qualifications and circumstances indicate a realistic ability to earn. Age, health, and the availability of employment are relevant in this assessment.

## Lump Sum vs Periodic Maintenance

The choice between lump-sum alimony and periodic maintenance has practical and tax consequences. A lump-sum payment made at the time of divorce provides finality and avoids the need for future enforcement. Under current income tax provisions, a lump-sum alimony payment is generally treated as a capital receipt in the hands of the recipient and is not taxable as income. Periodic maintenance, by contrast, is generally treated as income and is taxable in the hands of the recipient.

From the payer's perspective, a lump sum provides certainty, while periodic payments may be subject to future modification applications if circumstances change. Where the payer has liquid assets or a property that can be transferred, a lump-sum settlement is often the cleaner outcome.

## Modification of Maintenance Orders

A maintenance order is not final in the sense of being immune to change. Section 25(2) HMA and Section 144 BNSS both provide for modification on a change in circumstances. Material changes that courts recognise include a significant change in the income of either party (upward or downward), the remarriage of the claimant (which terminates the obligation under Section 25 HMA), the claimant becoming self-sufficient, or a material deterioration in the payer's financial position due to genuine reasons rather than deliberate impoverishment.

## Enforcement

A maintenance order from the Family Court can be enforced as a civil decree through execution proceedings, including attachment of the payer's salary, bank accounts, or property. An order under Section 144 BNSS is enforceable by the Magistrate through distress and, for persistent default, civil imprisonment of up to one month per month of default. Courts take non-payment of maintenance seriously and are willing to use coercive enforcement tools where the payer has the means to pay but wilfully refuses.

Maintenance is the obligation one spouse bears to support the other — and, where applicable, the children — when the marriage breaks down. The obligation can arise in multiple proceedings simultaneously, under different statutes with different courts and different standards. Understanding which route applies, and what courts in practice award, is the foundation of any realistic maintenance strategy.

## The statutory routes

### Section 24 HMA — interim maintenance during proceedings
Section 24 of the Hindu Marriage Act allows either spouse to apply for maintenance *pendente lite* (while proceedings are pending) and for the costs of the proceedings. The application can be made in any matrimonial proceeding — divorce, judicial separation, or restitution. The court looks at the applicant's income and the respondent's income and awards a sum for the pendency period. These awards are made quickly and are intended to put the parties on an equal footing during litigation.

### Section 25 HMA — permanent alimony
At the time of passing any decree under the Hindu Marriage Act, the court may award permanent alimony and maintenance payable by one spouse to the other. The amount is determined with reference to the respondent's income, the petitioner's own assets and earning capacity, the length of the marriage, the parties' conduct, and all other relevant circumstances. The court has a wide discretion.

Permanent alimony can be a lump sum, a periodic payment, or a combination. Courts increasingly favour lump-sum settlements, which bring finality. The order can subsequently be varied if circumstances change materially.

### Section 144 BNSS — maintenance before a Magistrate
The successor to Section 125 CrPC, Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita allows a wife (and minor children) to claim maintenance before a Magistrate — a criminal court. This route is quicker to initiate and has its own enforcement mechanisms (including civil prison for default). It applies regardless of religion. However, the quantum awarded is typically more modest than what Family Courts award under personal law statutes, and there is a cap on proceedings.

Many spouses use Section 144 BNSS proceedings alongside or in advance of matrimonial proceedings, as the Magistrate can move faster to an interim award.

### Domestic Violence Act 2005
The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act provides for a monetary relief order as part of the protection orders available to a woman in a domestic violence complaint. The magistrate can award maintenance covering the woman's living expenses, medical expenses, and losses, without waiting for divorce proceedings to conclude.

## Quantum: how courts assess maintenance

There is no statutory formula in India (unlike some other jurisdictions). Courts weigh:

**Needs of the claimant**: Living expenses, housing, medical needs, children's education and maintenance (separately assessed), and the ability to be self-sufficient.

**Means of the respondent**: Salary, business income, assets, liabilities, and obligations to other dependants. Courts look at actual income and, where there is evidence of suppression, imputed income.

**Standard of living during marriage**: This is a significant anchor. Courts expect the maintenance to allow the claimant to maintain a standard reasonably comparable to what they were accustomed to, adjusted for the fact that the household is now two.

**Length of marriage and age of the claimant**: A long marriage and a claimant who has been out of the workforce for years is treated differently from a short marriage where both parties are young and employed.

**Claimant's own earning capacity and resources**: Maintenance is not designed as permanent full support for an able-bodied adult who can work. Courts reduce or deny maintenance where the claimant has substantial independent income or assets.

## Enforcement

A maintenance order from a Family Court can be enforced as a civil decree — through execution of attachment of assets. An order under Section 144 BNSS can be enforced by the Magistrate through distress (attachment of property) and, for persistent default, civil imprisonment of up to one month per month of default.

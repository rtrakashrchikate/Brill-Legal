Custody decisions affect children at the most formative stages of their lives, and the law reflects this by placing the child's welfare — not parental rights — at the centre of every determination. Understanding how courts approach these decisions helps parents either reach sensible agreements themselves or prepare effectively for contested proceedings.

## The legal framework

Custody in India is governed by a combination of laws depending on the community:

- **Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains**: The Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act 1956 and the Guardians and Wards Act 1890
- **Muslims**: The Muslim Personal Law (though the welfare principle is overlaid by courts)
- **Christians and Parsis**: The Guardians and Wards Act 1890
- **All communities**: The Guardians and Wards Act 1890 provides the overarching framework for guardian appointments

Courts in matrimonial proceedings under the Hindu Marriage Act or the Special Marriage Act deal with custody as part of the divorce proceedings; the Family Court handles both together.

## Types of custody

### Physical custody
The parent with whom the child lives day-to-day has physical custody. It can be awarded solely to one parent, with defined access (visitation) to the other, or — in appropriate cases — shared, with the child spending substantial time with both parents.

### Legal custody
Legal custody is the authority to make decisions about the child's welfare: which school, what medical treatment, religious upbringing. Courts increasingly award joint legal custody (shared decision-making authority) even when only one parent has primary physical custody, recognising that both parents have a role in major decisions.

### Interim custody
During the pendency of proceedings — which can stretch for months or years — the court makes an interim custody order specifying where the child lives and the access arrangement. These orders balance stability (the child should not be shuffled between homes while proceedings drag on) with fairness to both parents.

## How courts decide contested custody

The welfare principle is applied through a set of practical factors:

**Age and needs of the child**: For very young children (particularly infants), courts have historically preferred the mother as primary carer, particularly in the tender years. This preference softens as the child grows older, and courts increasingly assess the individual circumstances.

**Stability of the home environment**: Employment, housing, extended family support, and the psychological environment each parent can provide are weighed.

**The child's existing attachments**: Disrupting an established primary attachment — to a parent, to a school, to a neighbourhood — carries a real welfare cost that courts are reluctant to impose without good reason.

**The non-custodial parent's access**: Courts look favourably on a primary carer who supports the child's relationship with the other parent. A parent who proposes to alienate the child from the other parent raises a welfare concern.

**The child's own wishes**: Courts take the views of older children (typically those above eight or ten) seriously, though they are not bound by them. A child's preference is one factor in the welfare assessment.

## International relocation

Where the custodial parent wishes to relocate abroad with the child, the court's permission is required if the other parent objects. India's non-signatory status to the Hague Convention means there is no automatic return mechanism for children brought to India from Hague countries, and Indian court orders directing a child's return to a Hague country are not automatically enforced abroad through treaty. These cases are complex and require specialist advice; our [family law practice](/practices/private-client) advises on international relocation and cross-border custody disputes.

## Enforcement of custody orders

Where a parent defies a custody order, the other parent can apply for contempt proceedings. Courts can impose fines, attach property, and in serious cases impose imprisonment. The police can also be directed to assist in recovering a child where the order is clear and the refusal to comply is wilful.

Custody orders are not immutable — a significant change in circumstances on either side can be the basis for a fresh application. The welfare principle governs both the original order and any variation.

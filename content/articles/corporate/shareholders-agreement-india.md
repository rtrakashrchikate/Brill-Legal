A shareholders' agreement (SHA) is the foundational private contract that governs the relationship between the owners of a company — whether a two-founder startup, a family-owned private company with outside investors, or a joint venture between established businesses. While the Companies Act 2013 and the company's Articles of Association set the legal framework within which a company operates, the SHA fills in the practical details: who controls what decisions, how equity is transferred, what happens when investors want to exit, and how disputes between shareholders are resolved. This guide explains the key elements of an Indian SHA and the practical considerations that arise in negotiating one.

## What a Shareholders' Agreement Covers

A comprehensive SHA for an Indian private limited company typically addresses:

**Governance and board composition:** How many directors the company will have, which shareholders have the right to appoint directors, what matters require board approval versus shareholder approval, and what decisions (if any) require the consent of a specific investor or class of shareholders — often referred to as "reserved matters" or "investor consent matters."

**Share capital and issuances:** The existing capitalisation, any agreed future rounds of investment, pre-emption rights (the right of existing shareholders to participate in new issuances before shares are offered to outsiders), and anti-dilution protections.

**Transfer restrictions:** The conditions under which shares can be transferred, including lock-in periods during which neither founders nor investors can sell, and the mechanisms that govern transfers after lock-in expires.

**Exit provisions:** Tag-along rights, drag-along rights, rights of first refusal (ROFR), and rights of first offer (ROFO), which together determine who can sell, to whom, and on what terms.

**Founder obligations:** Vesting schedules for founder shares, non-compete and non-solicitation obligations, and what happens to a founder's shares if they leave the company.

**Information rights:** The ongoing disclosure obligations to investors — quarterly management accounts, annual audited accounts, access to board materials, and the right to conduct periodic audits.

**Dispute resolution:** Whether disputes are resolved by arbitration (and if so, the seat, number of arbitrators, and governing rules) or litigation.

## SHA Versus Articles of Association: Understanding the Hierarchy

The Articles of Association (AoA) is a public document, filed with the Registrar of Companies and accessible by any member of the public. The SHA is a private contract between specific shareholders. This distinction has important legal consequences.

Provisions in the SHA that are inconsistent with the AoA may be unenforceable against the company — courts have held that the AoA, as the constitutional document, prevails. To ensure that SHA provisions (such as investor consent rights or transfer restrictions) are effective against the company and against future shareholders, the key provisions should either be incorporated into the AoA or the AoA should be amended to reference and give effect to the SHA.

In practice, startup investors often require that the AoA be amended at the time of investment to reflect the terms agreed in the SHA — particularly reserved matters, drag-along obligations, and transfer restrictions.

## Key Negotiating Points: Founders Versus Investors

### Founder Vesting

Vesting schedules for founder shares are now standard in investor-backed Indian startups. A typical four-year vesting schedule with a one-year cliff means that 25% of a founder's shares vest after one year of continuous employment, with the remaining 75% vesting monthly over the following three years. If a founder leaves before one year, they receive nothing; if they leave after two years, they keep the shares that have vested to date.

From a founder's perspective, the key negotiation points are: the treatment of unvested shares on exit (whether they are bought back at cost or at fair value), acceleration of vesting on certain exit events, and the definition of "good leaver" versus "bad leaver."

### Tag-Along and Drag-Along Rights

A tag-along right allows a minority shareholder to participate in a sale being made by the majority — "tagging along" on the same terms rather than being left behind. Drag-along rights allow the majority to compel the minority to sell their shares in a bona fide third-party acquisition — "dragging" dissenting minorities into a deal the majority has approved.

Founders typically want broad drag-along rights (to facilitate a clean exit for all shareholders) and meaningful tag-along thresholds (so that small investors cannot block a drag). Investors want tag-along rights that ensure they receive the same price as founders, and drag-along rights that include appropriate protections — such as minimum price floors or the ability to opt for a cash exit rather than share-for-share.

### ROFR and ROFO

A right of first refusal (ROFR) entitles the holder to purchase shares being sold by another shareholder at the same price and on the same terms as any third-party offer, after the third-party offer has been received. A right of first offer (ROFO) requires the selling shareholder to offer the shares to the ROFO holder first, before approaching third parties. ROFO is generally more favourable to the selling shareholder (less disclosure required to trigger it); ROFR is more favourable to the holder.

### Anti-Dilution Protection

Investors in Indian companies almost universally negotiate for anti-dilution protection — a mechanism that adjusts their conversion ratio if the company raises money at a lower valuation in a future round. The weighted average method, which moderates the adjustment based on the actual amount raised at the lower price, is the market standard. Full ratchet anti-dilution — which gives the investor a full adjustment regardless of the round size — is heavily resisted by founders and is rare in arm's length Indian startup transactions.

## Enforceability Under Indian Law

As a contract, the SHA is enforceable in India subject to general contract law principles — offer, acceptance, consideration, and the absence of grounds for avoidance (misrepresentation, undue influence, fraud). The key enforceability issues specific to SHAs in India are:

**Supremacy of the Companies Act:** Provisions that attempt to override mandatory requirements of the Companies Act 2013 — such as the rights of minority shareholders to petition NCLT under Sections 241–242 — are not given effect. These statutory rights cannot be contractually waived.

**Foreign investment regulations:** Where a shareholder is a foreign entity, the SHA must comply with the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) regulations on foreign direct investment. Provisions relating to guaranteed returns, exit at a pre-determined price, or other structures that effectively provide debt-like returns to foreign equity investors can raise FEMA compliance issues.

**Specific performance:** Breach of a SHA provision — say, a failure to exercise a drag-along right as required — may be remedied by an order of specific performance or an injunction, in addition to damages. Courts have granted specific performance of SHA obligations in appropriate cases.

## Common Disputes Prevented by Good Drafting

Many of the most destructive shareholder disputes in India arise from gaps or ambiguities in the SHA: undefined decision-making processes that lead to deadlock; vesting provisions that do not address what counts as a "good leaver" departure; transfer restrictions that do not clearly specify the process and timelines for ROFR exercises. A well-drafted SHA reduces the scope for dispute by addressing these scenarios explicitly, before the parties are adversarial.

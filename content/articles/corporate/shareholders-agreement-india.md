Every company with more than one shareholder has the potential for disagreement about how the company should be run, how profits should be distributed, how new capital should be raised, and what happens when a shareholder wants to exit. A shareholders' agreement (SHA) is the private contractual framework that answers these questions before they become disputes — establishing the rules of engagement for governance, investment, and exit in advance, when the parties still have the goodwill and mutual interest to agree on them. The importance of a well-drafted SHA cannot be overstated: it is the document that determines the balance of power in the company, the rights of minority investors, and the conditions under which any party can leave.

## SHA vs. Articles of Association: Understanding the Relationship

A shareholders' agreement and the company's Articles of Association (AoA) serve related but distinct functions. The AoA is a public document — registered with the Registrar of Companies, accessible to anyone — that governs the company's internal management. It binds the company and all its shareholders, whether or not they were party to its adoption. The SHA, by contrast, is a private contract between the parties who sign it. It does not automatically bind future shareholders unless they execute a deed of adherence joining the agreement, and it cannot directly bind the company unless the company is also a party.

The consequence of this distinction is significant: a governance right that exists only in the SHA but not in the AoA may be difficult to enforce against the company itself or against a new shareholder who joins the company. Courts have generally enforced SHA clauses between the original contracting parties as contractual obligations, awarding damages for breach. But specific performance — requiring the company to take a particular corporate action — is more complex if the AoA does not independently support it. Best practice, particularly for governance provisions and transfer restrictions, is to reflect the SHA's key terms in the AoA as well, ensuring that the two documents are aligned and that the rights have the force of both contractual and constitutional authority.

## Governance Rights

The governance provisions of an SHA typically address board composition, decision-making authority, and information rights. Investor shareholders — particularly institutional or private equity investors who hold a significant minority — will typically negotiate the right to appoint one or more nominee directors to the board. The number of nominee directors, their right to attend and vote at board meetings, and any entitlement to a seat on board committees (such as audit or compensation committees) should all be specified precisely, including the mechanics of appointment and removal.

Reserved matters are governance provisions that protect investors by requiring their specific approval — either at board level or at shareholder level — for certain high-impact decisions. Common reserved matters include approval of annual budgets and material deviations from them, borrowings above a specified threshold, acquisition or disposal of significant assets, entry into new lines of business, appointment and termination of key management personnel, related-party transactions, and changes to the AoA. The list should be tailored to the specific business and the investor's concerns rather than copied from a precedent without review. Overly long reserved matter lists can paralyse management decision-making; overly short ones fail to protect minority interests.

Information rights — the investor's entitlement to receive regular financial information, management accounts, board materials, and inspection rights — are a distinct and important category of governance protection. Without information rights, a minority shareholder cannot monitor the investment or detect problems early enough to respond.

## Share Transfer Restrictions

Transfer restrictions are among the most commercially important provisions of any SHA, governing who can become a shareholder in the company and on what terms. Founders are typically subject to lock-in periods — a defined period during which they cannot transfer their shares at all, intended to ensure that the people who built the business remain committed through the early growth phase. The lock-in period, its duration, and any carve-outs (such as transfers to family trusts or wholly-owned holding companies) must be clearly defined.

Beyond the lock-in, the right of first refusal (ROFR) is the most common transfer restriction in Indian SHAs. A ROFR requires a selling shareholder to offer their shares to the other shareholders — or to the company — at the same price and on the same terms as proposed by the third-party buyer, before completing the sale to the third party. A related but distinct mechanism is the right of first offer (ROFO), which requires the selling shareholder to offer the shares to other shareholders at a price they set, before the shares are marketed to third parties. Both mechanisms serve to give existing shareholders priority in acquiring shares before they reach the open market, but they operate differently and have distinct advantages and disadvantages that counsel should advise on.

## Tag-Along and Drag-Along Rights

Tag-along rights — also called co-sale rights — protect minority shareholders from finding themselves in a company controlled by a new majority owner they never chose. The mechanism is straightforward: if the majority shareholder agrees to sell their stake to a third party, the minority can require that the third-party buyer also acquire the minority's shares on the same terms (or at the same price per share, pro-rated). Without a tag-along, a buyer can acquire fifty-one percent of the company for a premium and leave the minority shareholders behind, now locked into a company with unfamiliar management and no route to liquidity.

Drag-along rights work in the opposite direction, protecting the majority's ability to complete a sale of the entire company. If the majority agrees to sell to a third party at a price that values the company fairly, they can require the minority to also sell their shares on the same terms — "dragging" the minority into the transaction. Without a drag-along, a minority shareholder can block a company sale by refusing to sell their shares, even if the majority and the proposed buyer have agreed on terms. Drag-along provisions typically specify a threshold — such as approval by shareholders holding a specified percentage — and a fair price mechanism to protect the minority from being dragged into an unfair transaction.

## Anti-Dilution Protection

Anti-dilution provisions protect existing investors from having the value of their shareholding reduced by the issuance of new shares at a price lower than the price they paid — a "down round." Two principal formulations are used in practice: full ratchet anti-dilution, which adjusts the investor's conversion price (in the case of convertible instruments) to the lower price of the new issue, without regard to the size of the new issue; and weighted average anti-dilution, which calculates an adjusted price that takes into account both the price of the new issue and the number of shares issued at that price, producing a less drastic adjustment. Weighted average anti-dilution is more commonly used in Indian venture transactions because it more fairly balances the investor's protection against the impact on founders and other shareholders.

Where a company has foreign investors, anti-dilution adjustments must be structured in compliance with FEMA pricing guidelines — which require that shares be issued to foreign investors at or above the fair market value determined by a prescribed methodology — meaning that full ratchet anti-dilution clauses that would result in the investor receiving additional shares below fair market value may conflict with FEMA requirements and require careful structuring.

## Exit Provisions

Exit provisions define the mechanisms through which investors can realise their investment. The most investor-friendly exit is an IPO obligation — a provision requiring the company and the founders to use best or reasonable efforts to achieve a public listing within a specified period after investment. Where an IPO is not feasible, strategic sale provisions or drag-along rights (discussed above) provide an alternative exit path.

Put options — the investor's right to require the founders or the company to buy back their shares at a specified price or formula — are used in some SHAs to provide a financial return floor. However, put options granted to foreign investors in Indian companies attract regulatory scrutiny: the Reserve Bank of India and FEMA guidelines restrict guaranteed returns on equity investments by foreign parties, and a put option that effectively guarantees the foreign investor a minimum return regardless of the company's performance may be held non-compliant. Structuring exit provisions in a way that complies with FEMA while providing the investor with adequate exit protection is a specialist task that requires careful legal and regulatory advice.

## Founder Vesting and Bad Leaver Provisions

Founder vesting — the mechanism by which a founder's shareholding is subject to forfeiture or buyback if they leave the company before the end of a vesting period — is a standard feature of investor-backed SHAs. A typical vesting schedule might provide for monthly vesting over four years, with a one-year cliff (meaning no shares vest in the first year, and 25% vest at the one-year mark). If a founder leaves before their shares have fully vested, the unvested shares are subject to repurchase by the company or other shareholders.

The bad leaver vs. good leaver distinction determines the price at which unvested shares are bought back. A good leaver — typically a founder who leaves for reasons such as ill-health, death, or termination without cause — is entitled to receive fair market value for their unvested shares. A bad leaver — a founder who is dismissed for cause (gross misconduct, breach of fiduciary duty, competing with the company) — typically receives only the nominal value of those shares, effectively forfeiting the economic benefit. The precise definitions of good leaver and bad leaver, and the price payable in each case, are highly negotiated provisions.

## FEMA and FDI Compliance

Any SHA involving a foreign shareholder in an Indian company must be structured with FEMA and FDI compliance as a primary consideration. The Foreign Exchange Management (Non-Debt Instruments) Rules 2019 govern the issue and transfer of shares to or by foreign nationals and non-residents. All share transfers to or from foreign parties must comply with the pricing guidelines prescribed by the RBI — shares cannot be issued to foreign investors below fair market value (determined by prescribed methodologies) and cannot be transferred by foreign investors below fair market value. Reporting requirements apply to the issuance, transfer, and buyback of shares involving foreign parties. Sector-specific caps on FDI — which apply to certain industries including defence, media, and insurance — may restrict the foreign investor's shareholding level.

Failure to comply with FEMA can result in contraventions that are subject to compounding by the RBI — a process by which the violation is regularised against payment of a compounding fee, but which requires disclosure and formal proceedings. Counsel advising on an SHA with foreign participation should conduct a thorough FEMA diligence review before the document is finalised.

## Enforceability and Dispute Resolution

Indian courts have generally upheld SHA clauses as binding contracts between sophisticated commercial parties, including granting specific performance in appropriate cases where damages would not be an adequate remedy. The choice of dispute resolution mechanism in the SHA — typically arbitration with a specified seat — should be consistent across all the transaction documents.

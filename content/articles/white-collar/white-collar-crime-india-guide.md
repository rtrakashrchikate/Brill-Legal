White-collar matters move differently from ordinary criminal cases. The investigation often starts long before an arrest — with a summons, a search, a freezing order or a notice — and the documents usually matter more than the witnesses. This guide explains the main offences, the agencies that investigate them, how a typical matter unfolds, and where the critical decision points sit. It is the anchor guide for our [white-collar crime and investigations practice](/practices/white-collar).

## What counts as white-collar crime in India

There is no single "white-collar crime" statute. The label covers offences scattered across several laws:

- **Cheating, criminal breach of trust and forgery** under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita — the workhorse provisions in most economic-offence FIRs.
- **Money laundering** under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 (PMLA), investigated by the Enforcement Directorate (ED).
- **Corporate fraud** under Section 447 of the Companies Act, 2013, investigated by the Serious Fraud Investigation Office (SFIO).
- **Securities offences** — insider trading, market manipulation, disclosure violations — under the SEBI Act and regulations.
- **Bank fraud** classified under RBI's framework and typically routed to the EOW or CBI.
- **Bribery and corruption** under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 (where a public servant is involved).
- **Foreign exchange violations** under FEMA — civil in character, but ED-enforced and frequently entangled with PMLA proceedings.

A single set of facts often attracts several of these at once. A bank-fraud allegation, for example, may produce a police FIR, an ED money-laundering case built on that FIR, and parallel proceedings before the [Debt Recovery Tribunal](/practices/tribunal).

## The agencies and how they differ

**Economic Offences Wing (EOW).** State police units for high-value frauds. They register FIRs, arrest, and file chargesheets like ordinary police — but the files are document-heavy and investigations run long.

**Enforcement Directorate (ED).** Investigates money laundering under PMLA. Distinct powers: it records statements under Section 50 (which, unlike police statements, are admissible), provisionally attaches property, and arrests under Section 19. PMLA cases require a "predicate offence" — an underlying scheduled offence registered by another agency.

**SFIO.** Investigates serious corporate fraud on central government reference. Its reports can lead to prosecution of companies, directors and auditors.

**SEBI.** A regulator with quasi-judicial powers — it passes disgorgement and debarment orders, settles through consent mechanisms, and can prosecute. Appeals lie to the Securities Appellate Tribunal.

**CBI.** Handles corruption involving central employees and matters referred by courts or consenting states.

## How a typical investigation unfolds

1. **The trigger.** A complaint, an audit flag, a regulator's reference, or an FIR by a counterparty or bank.
2. **Summons and statements.** Agencies summon persons of interest to produce documents and answer questions. ED statements under PMLA carry evidentiary weight — preparation matters.
3. **Searches and seizures.** Offices and residences may be searched; devices and records seized; bank accounts frozen.
4. **Attachment (PMLA).** The ED can provisionally attach property it considers "proceeds of crime"; the attachment goes before the Adjudicating Authority for confirmation, with an appeal to the Appellate Tribunal.
5. **Arrest and bail.** Arrest is not automatic — many matters proceed without it. Where it happens, bail strategy depends on the statute; [anticipatory bail](/insights/anticipatory-bail-india-guide) is often the critical early battle.
6. **Chargesheet / complaint and trial.** The agency files its final report; trial proceeds before the designated court (Special PMLA courts for money laundering).

Each stage has remedies: quashing petitions before the High Court, challenges to attachment, statutory appeals, and bail at multiple levels. Knowing [your rights on arrest](/insights/rights-on-arrest-india) before any of this starts is the best preparation.

## Defending a white-collar matter: what actually matters

**Documents first.** These cases are won and lost on paper — board minutes, banking trails, contemporaneous emails, audit working papers. Preserving and organising documents early is half the defence.

**Mind parallel proceedings.** The same facts may run before a criminal court, the ED, SEBI, the NCLT and a civil court simultaneously. Statements in one forum surface in another; strategy must be coordinated, not forum-by-forum.

**Procedure is substantive.** Whether a Section 50 summons was valid, whether arrest grounds were furnished in writing, whether attachment was confirmed in time — procedural points regularly decide white-collar cases. The Supreme Court has repeatedly tightened the requirement that the ED furnish written grounds of arrest.

**Companies need internal investigations.** When fraud surfaces inside an organisation, a privileged, well-scoped internal investigation protects the company — it establishes facts before regulators do and demonstrates good faith. We advise boards and audit committees on exactly this.

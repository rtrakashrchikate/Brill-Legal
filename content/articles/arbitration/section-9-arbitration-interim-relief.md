In commercial disputes, the period between the breakdown of relations and the final resolution of the dispute — whether by arbitral award, court judgment, or settlement — can be months or years. During that window, a party with an arguable claim can suffer real and sometimes irreversible harm: assets can be transferred, accounts can be emptied, businesses can be wound down, or critical contracts can be assigned away. Section 9 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996 is the legislative mechanism that allows parties to an arbitration agreement to approach the court for interim protection before, during, or after arbitral proceedings — without waiting for the tribunal's award.

## What Section 9 Provides

Section 9(1) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996 permits a party to an arbitration agreement to apply to the court for interim measures of protection before the commencement of the arbitral proceedings, during their pendency, or after the making of the award (but before it is enforced). The measures available include:

- **Interim injunctions** — restraining a party from taking specified actions, such as dealing with or transferring a disputed asset, making specific payments, or disclosing confidential information
- **Appointment of a receiver** — to take possession of and manage property or assets that are the subject of the dispute
- **Preservation orders** — directing that evidence, property, or assets be preserved pending the outcome of the arbitration
- **Attachment before judgment** — securing assets of the respondent to ensure that any future award can be satisfied
- **Any other interim measure of protection** as the court considers just and convenient

## When to Apply: Before, During, or After the Award

**Before commencement of arbitration:** A party can apply under Section 9 before issuing the notice invoking arbitration or before the tribunal is constituted. This is appropriate in genuinely urgent situations where waiting for the tribunal to be formed would cause irreparable harm. However, the 2015 amendment to the Act requires the applicant to have commenced arbitration within 90 days of a Section 9 order being passed (or within the time specified by the court), failing which the order lapses.

**During arbitration:** Once the arbitral tribunal is constituted, Section 9(3) provides that the court shall not entertain a fresh Section 9 application unless the applicant demonstrates that the tribunal's remedy under Section 17 (interim measures by the tribunal itself) would be inefficacious. In practice, most interim applications during a pending arbitration should be directed to the tribunal under Section 17, not the court.

**After the award:** A post-award Section 9 application is used to prevent the dissipation of assets during the window between the award being passed and its enforcement as a decree. Where the award debtor has made representations about assets or where enforcement is likely to be contested through a Section 34 challenge, post-award interim protection can preserve the value of the award.

## Which Court Has Jurisdiction

Following the Supreme Court's landmark BALCO decision (2012) and the 2015 legislative amendments, jurisdictional rules for arbitration-related court applications are determined by the seat, not the subject matter of the dispute. For a domestic arbitration with a Pune seat, Section 9 applications are filed before the Bombay High Court or the appropriate designated court in Pune — not in the courts of any other city simply because the contract was performed there or because the respondent's assets are located there.

For international commercial arbitrations seated in India, only the High Court of the seat has jurisdiction.

## The Legal Tests for Interim Relief

The court considering a Section 9 application applies the standard tests for interim injunctions established by the Supreme Court in *American Cyanamid* and adopted in India:

**Prima facie case:** The applicant must demonstrate that there is a serious question to be tried on the merits — not that success on the merits is certain, but that the claim is genuinely arguable.

**Balance of convenience:** The court weighs the harm to the applicant if relief is refused (and the applicant ultimately succeeds) against the harm to the respondent if relief is granted (and the respondent ultimately succeeds). Where the harm to the applicant significantly outweighs the harm to the respondent, the balance tips toward granting relief.

**Irreparable harm:** The applicant must show that damages alone — without the interim order — would not adequately compensate for the harm. Where the harm is the loss of money that can later be quantified and awarded, courts are less inclined to grant injunctions than where the harm is irreversible.

## Duty of Candour in Ex Parte Applications

When interim relief is urgently needed and the applicant seeks an order without notice to the respondent (ex parte), the applicant has a strict obligation of full and frank disclosure. Every material fact — including facts that weigh against granting the order — must be placed before the court. This is not merely a procedural nicety: failure to disclose material information is a standard ground for setting aside an ex parte interim order, and courts do not hesitate to impose costs or vacate relief where candour has been lacking.

## Practical Considerations

A well-prepared Section 9 application should be supported by an affidavit that sets out the factual background of the dispute concisely, identifies the arbitration agreement and the claimed breach, identifies the specific relief sought and the basis for it, explains why the situation is urgent and why the respondent should not be heard first if ex parte relief is sought, and exhibits the key documents — the agreement, correspondence showing the breach, and any evidence of the risk to assets.

The affidavit should be drafted with the legal tests in mind — each element of the prima facie case, balance of convenience, and irreparable harm addressed explicitly. Applications that leave the court to infer these elements from a narrative of grievances are less likely to succeed and more likely to generate contested hearings.

---

Section 9 is one of the most practically useful provisions in arbitration law — it gives parties the ability to ring-fence assets or prevent irreversible harm while the substantive dispute is resolved through the arbitral process. Used at the right moment and in the right way, it significantly improves the prospects of an award that is ultimately worth enforcing.

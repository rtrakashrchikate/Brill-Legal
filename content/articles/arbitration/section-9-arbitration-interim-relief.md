In any commercial dispute that is headed for arbitration, the period between the breakdown of the relationship and the eventual arbitral award can stretch from months to years. During that time, the very subject matter of the dispute — assets, funds, contractual rights, confidential information — may be dissipated, destroyed, or permanently altered by the opposing party. Interim relief under Section 9 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996 is the mechanism that allows a party to preserve the status quo, protect assets, and prevent irreparable harm while the arbitration is pending. In many disputes, the Section 9 application is the most urgent and strategically critical step — more consequential in practical terms than the ultimate arbitral award, which may arrive too late to remedy a harm that has already occurred.

## When Section 9 Applies

The statute provides three distinct windows during which a Section 9 application may be filed, each serving a different tactical purpose. The first window opens before the commencement of arbitral proceedings — meaning before a notice of arbitration has been issued or the tribunal has been constituted. This pre-arbitration application is the most urgent: a party who discovers that the counterparty is dissipating assets, destroying evidence, or dealing with the subject matter of the contract in a way that will frustrate any eventual award can approach the court immediately, without waiting for the arbitration machinery to be set up. The court may act on an ex parte basis in genuinely urgent cases, giving relief within days.

The second window runs during the arbitral proceedings — from the commencement of arbitration until the tribunal issues its final award. During this period, both the court under Section 9 and the tribunal under Section 17 have concurrent jurisdiction to pass interim measures, subject to the important limitation introduced by the 2015 Amendment (discussed below). The third window opens after the tribunal has issued an award but before the award is enforced as a decree. This post-award Section 9 is particularly useful where the award-debtor has assets that could be dissipated during the period between the award and its conversion into an executable decree.

## What Orders Can a Court Make Under Section 9

The range of orders available under Section 9 is deliberately broad. A court may pass an injunction restraining a party from alienating, encumbering, or dealing with a specific property or asset pending the arbitration — the classic freezing or Mareva injunction in the arbitration context. It may order the attachment before judgment of property or funds, ensuring that the award-creditor has a target from which to recover once the award is made. Where goods or perishable assets are in dispute, the court may appoint a receiver to take custody and manage the assets. The court may also order a party to deposit a specified sum in court or in a designated escrow — a useful order where the dispute involves a liquidated claim and the risk of non-payment is real.

More broadly, Section 9 empowers the court to pass "any other interim measure of protection as may appear to the court to be just and convenient" — a residual power that has been interpreted broadly to cover orders for disclosure of assets, orders preventing transfer of intellectual property rights, and orders restraining a party from taking steps in foreign jurisdictions that would prejudice the Indian arbitration.

## Which Court Has Jurisdiction

Jurisdiction under Section 9 is determined by the seat of arbitration, following the Supreme Court's landmark ruling in BGS SGS Soma JV v. NHPC Ltd (2019) and the foundational analysis in Bharat Aluminium Co. v. Kaiser Aluminium (BALCO, 2012). The court at the seat has exclusive supervisory jurisdiction over the arbitration — including the power to pass Section 9 orders, to entertain appointment applications under Section 11, and to hear Section 34 challenges to the award. Where the seat is Mumbai, applications must be filed before the Bombay High Court or the appropriate Commercial Court in Mumbai; where the seat is Delhi, the Delhi High Court or the Delhi Commercial Court has jurisdiction. This is so regardless of where the contract was performed, where the cause of action arose, or where the defendant is located. Choosing the seat of arbitration — a decision typically made when the contract is drafted — therefore has direct practical consequences for where interim relief applications must be filed and heard.

## The Three Tests for Interim Relief

A court asked to grant interim relief under Section 9 applies the well-established three-pronged test: first, whether the applicant has a prima facie case on the merits — not a proven case, but one that raises serious questions going to trial; second, whether the balance of convenience favours granting relief — whether the potential harm to the applicant from refusing relief outweighs the harm to the respondent from granting it; and third, whether the applicant will suffer irreparable harm or injury that cannot be adequately compensated in damages if relief is refused.

In the context of arbitration, the prima facie test does not require the court to make a final determination on the merits of the claim — that is for the tribunal. It requires only that the claim is not frivolous and has a genuine legal basis. The balance of convenience analysis looks holistically at the consequences of granting or refusing the order: an order freezing a party's bank account that constitutes all its working capital may be devastating to the respondent's business, even if the applicant's claim is genuine, and courts are sensitive to the proportionality of the relief. The irreparable harm inquiry asks whether an eventual award in the applicant's favour will provide a complete remedy, or whether by then the harm will be irreversible — such as where assets are being dissipated, third-party rights have been created, or the subject matter of the contract will cease to exist.

## Duty of Candour and Ex Parte Applications

Where the urgency of the situation requires an ex parte hearing — relief granted without notice to the other party — the applicant owes the court an absolute duty of full and frank disclosure. Every material fact known to the applicant, including facts that may be adverse to the application, must be placed before the court. This duty is not limited to legal issues: it extends to all factual matters that the court would want to know in deciding whether to grant relief. Failure to comply with this duty — concealing a prior failed application, not disclosing a material change of circumstances, or presenting a one-sided picture of events — can result in the ex parte order being vacated, costs being imposed on the applicant, and adverse inferences drawn in the main arbitration. Any ex parte order passed under Section 9 must be followed by notice to the respondent and an opportunity to be heard at the earliest practicable date.

## Relationship Between Section 9 and Section 17 (Tribunal's Power)

Section 17 of the Arbitration Act confers on the arbitral tribunal itself the power to pass interim measures — a power substantially expanded by the 2015 Amendment to bring it in line with Section 9. Once the arbitral tribunal has been constituted, Section 9(3) — introduced by the 2015 Amendment — provides that a court shall not entertain a Section 9 application "unless it finds that circumstances exist which may not render the remedy provided under Section 17 efficacious." In practice, this means that once a tribunal is in place and functioning, interim relief applications should generally be directed to the tribunal under Section 17 rather than to the court, unless the specific circumstances make tribunal relief inadequate — such as where the relief requires enforcement against third parties who are not before the tribunal, or where the tribunal's location makes urgent relief impractical.

## Post-Award Section 9: Protecting Enforcement

The post-award window for Section 9 applications — available after the award is made but before enforcement — fills an important gap. An arbitral award is not self-executing: it must be converted into an executable decree through the court process, and that conversion takes time. During this interval, an award-debtor who has lost the arbitration may attempt to dispose of assets, transfer them to connected parties, or otherwise render the award a paper victory. A post-award Section 9 application seeking an injunction against asset disposal or an attachment of specific assets can preserve the enforceability of the award. The court's analysis in a post-award Section 9 proceeds on the basis that the award has already determined the parties' rights — the prima facie test is therefore easily met — and the focus shifts to the balance of convenience and the risk of dissipation.

## Practical Steps for Filing a Section 9 Application

Filing a Section 9 application requires a petition setting out the facts of the dispute, the arbitration agreement, the specific relief sought, and the grounds — prima facie case, balance of convenience, and irreparable harm — explained by reference to the evidence. An affidavit in support, exhibiting the key documents (the contract, the arbitration clause, any correspondence, and evidence of the acts or threatened acts giving rise to urgency), accompanies the petition. In genuinely urgent matters, an application for immediate listing can be made to the court's urgent mention board. Where ex parte relief is sought, the affidavit should address the duty of disclosure explicitly. Courts in India's commercial courts and High Courts are generally responsive to well-prepared Section 9 applications supported by clear evidence of urgency.

Arbitration is how Indian businesses increasingly resolve commercial disputes — privately, before a chosen tribunal, on a statutory clock. Done well, it is faster and more commercially literate than court litigation; done badly, it adds a layer of cost before the court fight it was meant to avoid. The difference usually lies in decisions made long before any dispute: the clause, the seat, the institution. This guide walks the full arc. It anchors our [arbitration and ADR practice](/practices/arbitration).

## When arbitration applies

Arbitration rests on **agreement**. If your contract contains an arbitration clause — or you both agree after the dispute arises — the dispute goes to arbitration, and courts will refer it there if either party insists (Section 8, Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996).

Some disputes are **non-arbitrable** regardless of the clause: criminal offences, matrimonial status, insolvency, tenancy under rent-control statutes, and disputes affecting rights *in rem* like title to land. Fraud allegations, after years of uncertainty, are now generally arbitrable unless they strike at the arbitration agreement itself or have public ramifications.

## The clause: where most problems are born

A workable arbitration clause settles five things:

1. **Seat** — the legal home of the arbitration, which determines the supervising court. Seat is not venue; the words matter.
2. **Number of arbitrators** — one for ordinary contracts, three for high-value disputes.
3. **Institution or ad hoc** — institutional rules (MCIA, DIAC, SIAC, ICC) supply appointment machinery, timelines and fee schedules; ad hoc arbitration leaves these to the parties and, on failure, the courts.
4. **Language and law** — explicit, always.
5. **Pre-arbitration steps** — negotiation or mediation tiers, drafted so they cannot be weaponised for delay.

We draft and audit clauses constantly in our [commercial contracts work](/practices/corporate); the cost of a careful clause is an hour, and the cost of a careless one can be a year of jurisdictional skirmishing.

## Starting the arbitration and constituting the tribunal

Arbitration begins with a **Section 21 notice** invoking the clause. If the other side will not cooperate in appointing the tribunal, a **Section 11 application** asks the High Court (or Supreme Court for international matters) to appoint — and after the 2019 amendments, that examination is confined to whether an arbitration agreement exists.

Arbitrators must be independent: the Act's schedules, borrowing from the IBA Guidelines, disqualify persons with listed relationships to parties or counsel. The era of employer-appointed sole arbitrators in standard-form contracts is over.

## Interim relief: Section 9 and emergency arbitrators

Disputes rarely wait for a tribunal. **Section 9** lets a party seek interim protection from court — freezing assets, securing the amount in dispute, preserving goods — before or during arbitration. Once the tribunal exists, **Section 17** gives it the same powers, and its orders are enforceable as court orders.

Institutional rules add **emergency arbitrators** for relief within days; following the Supreme Court's *Amazon v. Future* ruling, emergency awards in India-seated arbitrations are enforceable. The choreography between Section 9 and the tribunal is tactical terrain where experienced counsel earn their keep — much as with [injunctions in civil suits](/insights/civil-litigation-india-guide).

## The hearing and the award

Arbitration procedure is flexible: statements of claim and defence, document disclosure scaled to the dispute, witness statements, cross-examination, written and oral argument. The Act demands awards within **twelve months** of pleadings closing (extendable six months by consent), and fee schedules are statutory for ad hoc tribunals.

The award must state reasons, decide interest and costs, and is stamped like a decree. Costs now presumptively **follow the event** — the loser pays — a real discipline on speculative claims.

## Challenging an award: Section 34

An award can be set aside only on narrow grounds: invalid agreement, lack of notice, decisions beyond the reference, illegal tribunal composition, non-arbitrability, or conflict with the **public policy of India** — which, after the 2015 amendments, means fraud, contravention of fundamental policy, or basic morality and justice, and expressly **not** a merits review. "Patent illegality" is an additional ground for purely domestic awards. The application must be filed within **three months** (plus a maximum 30-day grace) — a hard limit.

A Section 34 challenge does **not** automatically stay enforcement; the challenger must seek a stay and will usually be ordered to deposit a substantial portion of the award.

## Enforcement — domestic and foreign

Once the challenge window closes or the challenge fails, a domestic award is **executed as a decree** — attachment, garnishee orders, the full machinery. Foreign awards from New York Convention territories are enforced under Part II; resistance is confined to treaty defences, and Indian courts now apply them narrowly. Where the award-debtor is insolvent, enforcement intersects with proceedings before the [NCLT](/practices/tribunal).

## Mediation and conciliation

Not every dispute needs an award. The Mediation Act, 2023 and court-annexed mediation give settlements the force of decrees, and commercial courts require **pre-institution mediation** for many suits.

An arbitration clause is the provision in a commercial contract that determines how disputes will be resolved — and, if disputes arise, it becomes the most important clause in the entire agreement. A well-drafted clause provides certainty, reduces satellite litigation, and gives the arbitral tribunal a clear framework to work within. A poorly drafted clause — sometimes called a "pathological" clause — can create confusion about jurisdiction, delay proceedings, and generate costly court battles over procedural points before the merits of the dispute are ever addressed. This guide is intended to help parties and their advisors think through the key decisions that go into an effective arbitration clause.

## The Essential Elements

Every arbitration clause in an Indian commercial contract should address, at minimum, six elements:

1. **Agreement to arbitrate** — a clear statement that all or specified disputes shall be referred to arbitration, rather than to courts
2. **Seat of arbitration** — the legal place of arbitration, which determines which jurisdiction's courts have supervisory power and which procedural law governs
3. **Number and appointment of arbitrators** — whether the tribunal is a sole arbitrator or a panel of three, and how they are appointed (by mutual agreement, by an institution, or by the court under Section 11 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act)
4. **Governing law** — both the law governing the substantive contract and the law governing the arbitration procedure (which follows the seat)
5. **Language** — the language in which the proceedings will be conducted
6. **Institutional or ad hoc** — whether the arbitration is administered by an institution or conducted on an ad hoc basis

## Choosing the Seat

The seat is the single most consequential decision in an arbitration clause because it determines which court has supervisory jurisdiction — to hear challenges to the arbitral tribunal, to grant interim relief before an award is made, and to entertain applications to set aside the award. For a purely domestic contract between Indian parties, designating an Indian city as the seat (Mumbai, Delhi, Bengaluru, Pune) is standard. The courts at the seat have exclusive jurisdiction over such applications.

For cross-border contracts — particularly those involving foreign parties — the choice between an Indian seat and a neutral international seat (Singapore, London, Dubai) is a substantive negotiation. An Indian seat means the award is domestic and subject to challenge under Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act; a foreign seat means the award is a foreign award, enforced under the New York Convention provisions in Sections 44–58. Neither is inherently superior — the choice depends on the parties' relationship, the nature of the contract, and the practical enforceability considerations.

## Specifying the Number of Arbitrators

For most commercial disputes, a sole arbitrator reduces cost and time. A tribunal of three arbitrators — one appointed by each party and a presiding arbitrator appointed by the two — is appropriate for high-stakes or complex disputes where the cost is justified by the complexity and the amounts at stake. The clause should specify the number; if it does not, the Act defaults to a sole arbitrator for domestic arbitrations under Section 10.

The appointment mechanism must be workable. A clause that says "the parties shall mutually appoint an arbitrator" without a fallback creates a loophole — if one party refuses to cooperate, the other must apply to the court under Section 11, which adds delay. A well-drafted clause either designates an institution to make the appointment, specifies a process with time limits, or includes a clear fallback to court appointment.

## Institutional Versus Ad Hoc Arbitration

**Institutional arbitration** means the proceedings are administered by a designated arbitral institution — such as the Mumbai Centre for International Arbitration (MCIA), the Indian Council of Arbitration (ICA), the Singapore International Arbitration Centre (SIAC), or the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC). The institution provides a secretariat, default rules for appointment and challenges, an administered process for exchanging submissions, and in some cases a review of the award for formal defects before publication. The institutional rules become part of the parties' agreement by reference. Administrative fees are charged.

**Ad hoc arbitration** under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996 (as amended) is self-administered — the parties and arbitrators manage the procedural steps themselves, using the Act's default provisions and any additional rules the parties agree upon. It is more flexible and has lower administrative overhead, but requires more direct cooperation between the parties at the procedural stage.

## Tiered Dispute Resolution

Many sophisticated commercial contracts include a tiered or multi-step dispute resolution clause that requires the parties to attempt resolution through negotiation — and sometimes mediation — before arbitration can be invoked. A typical structure might require:

- **Step 1:** Written notice of dispute to the other party, followed by 30 days of good faith negotiation between senior management representatives
- **Step 2:** If negotiation fails, referral to a neutral mediator for a further 30 days
- **Step 3:** If mediation fails or is declined, arbitration

For a tiered clause to work effectively, the pre-conditions to arbitration must be clearly defined — so that it is unambiguous when the negotiation or mediation step has been completed or has failed — and the time periods must be proportionate so that the process does not become an obstacle to accessing arbitration in urgent situations.

## Common Pathological Clause Mistakes

**Failure to specify the seat:** A clause that says arbitration shall take place "at such place as the parties may agree" or that names a venue without specifying it as the seat creates jurisdictional uncertainty. Indian courts have had to determine the seat — and therefore the supervisory court — from surrounding circumstances, which is an expensive exercise.

**Contradictory seat and court jurisdiction clauses:** A clause that specifies arbitration at Pune but also says "the courts at Delhi shall have exclusive jurisdiction" is self-contradictory. Courts have varied in how they resolve this conflict, and the uncertainty benefits no one.

**Unilateral arbitrator appointment:** A clause giving one party — particularly the stronger party in a contract — the sole right to appoint the arbitrator has been held by the Supreme Court to be legally problematic. It undermines the independence of the process.

**Overly broad or narrow scope:** A clause that arbitrates "any dispute arising out of this agreement" is usually broad enough. Clauses that try to carve out specific types of disputes — such as claims for urgent injunctive relief — without careful drafting can create confusion about what is and is not covered.

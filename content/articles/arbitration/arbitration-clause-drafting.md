Commercial contracts are negotiated with the hope that they will never be disputed, but experienced counsel and their clients know that the arbitration clause — the provision specifying how disputes will be resolved — is in some respects the most important clause in the entire agreement. It is the one clause that becomes operative precisely when the rest of the contract has broken down. A well-drafted arbitration clause channels disputes to a predictable, enforceable forum and eliminates jurisdictional uncertainty. A poorly drafted one — even a single carelessly chosen word — can generate preliminary litigation about where and how to arbitrate before any substantive dispute is even heard, consuming time and money that the parties sought to avoid by including an arbitration clause in the first place.

## The Essential Elements of an Arbitration Clause

Every effective arbitration clause must address a core set of elements, each of which can generate significant problems if left ambiguous or omitted. The seat of arbitration is the legal domicile of the proceedings — it determines which country's or state's courts have supervisory jurisdiction, which procedural law applies, and the validity requirements for any eventual award. The venue is the physical location where hearings take place and carries no independent legal significance, but the failure to distinguish between seat and venue in the clause is one of the most persistent sources of jurisdictional disputes in Indian arbitration.

The number of arbitrators — typically one or three — must be specified, along with the mechanism for appointment. A clause that provides for three arbitrators and gives each party the right to nominate one, with the two party-nominated arbitrators jointly appointing the third, is straightforward and commonly used. A clause that provides for appointment by mutual agreement without a fallback mechanism is asking for trouble, since parties in dispute will rarely agree on anything — including who should arbitrate the dispute. The governing law of the contract (which law governs the substantive rights and obligations) and the language of arbitration should also be stated. For agreements between Indian parties, this will usually be Indian law and English, but for contracts with cross-border elements the governing law choice warrants specific thought.

## Seat vs. Venue: The Critical Distinction

The significance of the seat-venue distinction in Indian law was authoritatively settled by the Supreme Court in BGS SGS Soma JV v. NHPC Ltd (2019), which affirmed and clarified the earlier ruling in BALCO v. Kaiser Aluminium (2012). The court held that the designation of a seat in an arbitration agreement confers exclusive supervisory jurisdiction on the courts of that seat, regardless of where the cause of action arose or where the contract was performed. The practical consequence is significant: if the seat is specified as Mumbai, the Bombay High Court or the Commercial Court in Mumbai has exclusive jurisdiction to entertain applications under Section 9 (interim relief), Section 34 (challenge to the award), and any other supervisory application under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996.

Clauses that specify a "venue" without using the word "seat" — or that use both terms to refer to different locations — have generated extensive preliminary litigation about which court has jurisdiction. The safest drafting practice is to use the word "seat" explicitly, to give it a specific city, and to separately specify that hearings may be conducted at the seat or at such other venue as the parties may agree — making clear that the venue for any particular hearing does not alter the seat. A sample formulation might read: "The seat of arbitration shall be Mumbai, India. Hearings may be conducted at such location as the tribunal and parties may agree from time to time."

## Institutional vs. Ad Hoc Arbitration

The choice between institutional and ad hoc arbitration is one of the most consequential decisions in the clause. Institutional arbitration — conducted under the rules and administration of a recognised arbitration institution — provides a framework for appointment of arbitrators (including a default mechanism if parties fail to agree), procedural rules that govern timelines and submissions, administrative support, and in some cases scrutiny of the draft award before it is issued. Major Indian institutions include the Mumbai Centre for International Arbitration (MCIA), the International Centre for Alternative Dispute Resolution (ICADR), and the Delhi International Arbitration Centre (DIAC). Major international institutions whose rules are commonly chosen for India-related disputes include the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC), the Singapore International Arbitration Centre (SIAC), and the London Court of International Arbitration (LCIA).

Ad hoc arbitration, conducted without institutional oversight, gives parties maximum flexibility in designing their own procedure, can be cost-effective in lower-value disputes, and avoids institutional fees. However, it requires the parties — and ultimately the arbitrators — to manage all procedural issues themselves, and a breakdown in co-operation between the parties (which is common in adversarial disputes) can cause significant delays. For any dispute where the amounts at stake are substantial or where the counterparty's co-operation cannot be assumed, institutional arbitration provides a more reliable framework.

## Multi-Tier Dispute Resolution Clauses

Many commercial contracts benefit from a multi-tier dispute resolution clause — a structured process that requires the parties to attempt negotiation or mediation before invoking arbitration. A typical tiered clause might provide that disputes must first be referred to the senior management of each party for resolution within a thirty-day negotiation period, and that if unresolved within that period, either party may refer the dispute to arbitration. Some clauses add a mandatory mediation step between negotiation and arbitration, specifying a recognised mediation institution and a defined mediation period.

The advantage of a tiered clause is that it creates opportunities for commercial resolution before the adversarial costs of arbitration are incurred, while preserving access to arbitration as a backstop. The critical drafting requirement is specificity — each tier must state a clear trigger, a defined time period, and an unambiguous next step. A tiered clause that says disputes "should be resolved through discussions" before arbitration, without specifying timelines, has been held by courts to be insufficiently clear to constitute a mandatory pre-condition to arbitration, defeating the purpose of the tier.

## Common Drafting Mistakes

Several drafting errors recur with sufficient frequency to warrant explicit attention. Naming a non-existent institution — such as "the Mumbai International Arbitration Centre" rather than the Mumbai Centre for International Arbitration (MCIA) — can render the institutional reference inoperative, leaving parties with ad hoc arbitration governed by the institution's rules in name only. Failing to specify a seat leaves the court with jurisdiction to determine the seat based on the surrounding facts — an exercise that generates litigation and may produce a result neither party wanted.

Mixing the governing law of the contract (Indian law) with the governing law of the arbitration agreement (which may be different) without intention is a common oversight in cross-border transactions. Specifying an appointment mechanism that requires both parties' agreement without a fallback to an appointing authority or court creates a deadlock whenever the parties are not co-operating — precisely the condition in which appointment is most needed. Including an arbitration clause in one part of the contract and a jurisdiction clause in another, without reconciling the two, creates ambiguity about whether disputes must be arbitrated or litigated. Each of these errors is avoidable with careful drafting and review.

## Sample Language to Discuss With Counsel

A starting-point institutional arbitration clause for an India-seated domestic transaction might read as follows: "Any dispute arising out of or in connection with this Agreement, including any question regarding its existence, validity, or termination, shall be referred to and finally resolved by arbitration under the [Institution] Rules, which rules are deemed to be incorporated by reference into this clause. The seat of arbitration shall be [City], India. The tribunal shall consist of [one/three] arbitrator[s]. The language of the arbitration shall be English. The governing law of this Agreement shall be the laws of India." This template should be tailored to the specific transaction — the choice of institution, the number of arbitrators (one is more economical for smaller disputes, three provides more deliberative depth for complex ones), and any specific procedural variations relevant to the agreement. Counsel should review and adapt any template before use.

## International Contracts: Additional Considerations

Where one party is foreign, the choice of seat takes on greater significance. A foreign seat — Singapore, London, or another established arbitration jurisdiction — means the arbitration is governed by Part II of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996 rather than Part I, which significantly limits Indian court supervision. The award, if obtained, would be a foreign award enforceable under the New York Convention, and enforcement in India would proceed under Sections 44 to 58 of the Act. Payments of arbitrator fees, institutional fees, and counsel fees to parties outside India may require RBI compliance under FEMA. For Indian subsidiaries of multinational groups with group-wide arbitration agreements, the governing law and seat may be fixed at the group level — but the Indian entity's counsel should verify that the clause works under Indian law before the contract is signed.

Redevelopment of ageing cooperative housing societies has become one of the most consequential and frequently contested processes in Maharashtra's real estate landscape. For members of a society — often people who have lived in the same building for decades — redevelopment involves significant legal, financial, and personal considerations. The legal framework governing the process involves multiple overlapping statutes, and the terms of the Development Agreement signed with a developer can have lasting consequences for every member. This guide explains the key legal concepts every society member should understand before their general body meeting votes on a redevelopment proposal.

## The Legal Framework

Cooperative housing society redevelopment in Maharashtra operates under several laws simultaneously:

- The **Maharashtra Cooperative Societies Act 1960** and the Rules thereunder, which govern the society's internal decision-making and the relationship between the management committee and members
- The **Development Control and Promotion Regulations (DCPR 2034)**, which set the Floor Space Index (FSI) available for redevelopment and the procedural conditions for municipal approval
- The **Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act 2016**, which applies to the developer's component of the project (the additional units sold to fund construction)
- The **Maharashtra Ownership Flats Act 1963** and the draft model agreement, which may be relevant depending on the documentation structure

## Consent Thresholds: 51% Versus 70%

The decision to redevelop requires the approval of members at a properly convened special general body meeting. The general rule under Maharashtra law requires at least a 51% majority of members. However, government circulars and judicial interpretations have in practice pushed toward a 70% or higher consensus in many cases, particularly where individual members object on grounds of financial inadequacy of the offer or procedural irregularity.

The process matters as much as the numbers. The notice for the general body meeting must correctly state the agenda; members must be given sufficient time to review the proposed Development Agreement and the valuations; and the meeting must be quorate. Irregularities in the consent process are one of the most common grounds on which dissenting members successfully challenge redevelopment approvals before the Co-operative Court.

## The Development Agreement: Key Clauses

The Development Agreement (DA) between the society and the developer is the central legal document of the redevelopment transaction. Members should insist on reviewing the full draft — not merely a summary — before voting. Key clauses to scrutinise include:

**Carpet area guarantee:** The DA must specify the minimum carpet area of the new flat each member will receive. Ideally, this should be equal to or greater than the existing carpet area (not built-up or super built-up area). Any condition that allows the developer to reduce the flat's area below this guarantee, or to substitute a different location or floor without the member's consent, should be resisted.

**Transit rent:** Members who vacate their flats during construction must be paid transit rent — a monthly amount sufficient to rent comparable accommodation in the vicinity. The DA should specify the transit rent per flat per month (indexed to the actual rental market, not a fixed nominal amount), the date of commencement, and a clear mechanism for escalation if construction is delayed.

**Construction timeline:** The DA must state a clear, time-bound construction schedule with intermediate milestones and a binding date for handing over the new units to members. RERA registration of the project provides an additional backstop by making the declared completion date a public, regulatory commitment.

**Corpus fund:** The one-time corpus fund paid by the developer to the society should be quantified in the DA — not left to later negotiation. An independent valuer's assessment of the appropriate corpus amount is advisable.

**Quality specifications:** The specifications for the new building — materials, fittings, lobby quality, parking, and amenities — should be annexed to the DA as a schedule and should be sufficiently detailed to be enforceable. Vague references to "standard construction" allow developers to cut costs at the delivery stage.

**Alternative accommodation or bank guarantee:** The DA should require the developer to provide alternative accommodation or a bank guarantee to secure the transit rent and corpus fund obligations, so that members are not entirely dependent on the developer's goodwill if the project is delayed.

## Member Rights to Audit and Information

Under the Maharashtra Cooperative Societies Act, every member of a cooperative housing society has the right to inspect the society's books and records, including the terms of any Development Agreement entered into by the society. Members also have the right to appoint an independent auditor to review the terms of the deal and the society's accounts. Any management committee that stonewalls legitimate requests for documents from members during a redevelopment process is in breach of its statutory obligations.

Members who were not given adequate notice of the special general body meeting, who were denied access to the draft DA before the vote, or who believe the management committee acted in bad faith can approach the Registrar of Cooperative Societies or the Co-operative Court for relief.

## RERA's Role in Redevelopment Projects

When the developer's component — the additional units sold in the open market to fund the redevelopment — exceeds the RERA threshold (more than eight units or more than 500 square metres of covered area), the project must be registered with MahaRERA. This registration gives members of the society the protections of RERA allottees, including the right to claim interest or refund under Section 18 if the new flats are not handed over on time.

Practical implication: the developer's declared possession date on MahaRERA becomes the members' benchmark. If the new flats are delivered late, members can file a complaint — just as any other homebuyer would — and claim interest for the delay period.

## When to Seek Legal Advice

Legal advice is particularly important at three stages of the redevelopment process:

1. **Before the general body meeting vote** — to review the draft DA, assess whether the proposed corpus fund and transit rent are adequate, identify missing protections, and advise on how to exercise member rights if the proposal is inadequate
2. **After the vote, before execution** — to negotiate specific changes to the DA before it is formally signed, while there is still leverage
3. **During or after construction** — if the developer is delaying, deviating from approved plans, failing to pay transit rent, or otherwise breaching the DA

Redevelopment offers society members the prospect of better-quality housing and improved facilities, but the legal and financial risks in a poorly structured deal are real. The Development Agreement, once signed, is difficult to renegotiate. Taking legal advice before the vote is taken — not after — is the most effective way to protect members' interests.

Housing society redevelopment has become one of the most significant legal and financial decisions facing members of ageing co-operative housing societies across Mumbai and Pune. Decades-old buildings, many of them structurally compromised and obsolete by modern standards, occupy prime urban land that — under the increased Floor Space Index (FSI) permitted by revised development control regulations — can support much larger buildings. The arithmetic is attractive: members get brand-new, larger flats; the developer gets additional saleable area to fund the project and generate profit. But the gap between that promised arithmetic and the actual outcome of a poorly managed redevelopment is enormous, and the risks to members who do not understand their legal position are severe.

## The Legal Framework

Redevelopment in Maharashtra operates at the intersection of several overlapping statutes and regulations. The Maharashtra Co-operative Societies Act 1960 (MCS Act) governs the internal working of co-operative housing societies — their management, meetings, resolutions, and disputes — and provides the framework within which members vote on and authorise redevelopment. The Development Control and Promotion Regulations 2034 (DCPR 2034) applicable in the Mumbai municipal area govern the permissible FSI, the incentives available for redevelopment of old cessed buildings, and the procedures for obtaining planning permission.

The Maharashtra government has also issued a series of Government Resolutions specifically addressing self-redevelopment — a model under which the society itself acts as the developer, financed by bank loans, without appointing a private developer. Section 79A of the MCS Act gives the State government power to issue directions to co-operative societies, and various circulars under this power have standardised the minimum terms that a development agreement must contain. The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act 2016 applies once a project crosses the registration threshold, adding a layer of transparency and escrow protection for all units in the redeveloped building, not just those being allotted to existing members.

## Consent Requirements: The SGM Process

The redevelopment process formally begins when a minimum percentage of members initiate the process by calling a Special General Meeting (SGM). The notice convening the SGM must be issued with proper advance notice — typically fourteen clear days — and must specify redevelopment as an agenda item. Quorum requirements under the MCS Act must be satisfied for the meeting to be valid. At the SGM, a resolution in favour of redevelopment must pass with the consent of a minimum of 51% of all members of the society — not just those present at the meeting — making it important that the society actively encourages attendance rather than relying on a narrow majority of those who happen to appear.

The resolution must be properly recorded in the minutes of the meeting, signed by the chairperson, and submitted to the Registrar of Co-operative Societies within the prescribed period. Members who were not present at the SGM or who voted against the resolution are not bound to vacate their premises without being party to the development agreement or receiving the protections it is supposed to provide. A resolution that is later found to be procedurally defective — wrong notice period, insufficient quorum, inadequate majority, or failure to disclose material information about the proposed terms — can be challenged before the Co-operative Court.

## Selecting the Developer

Once the SGM authorises redevelopment in principle, the society must follow a transparent process to select a developer. The recommended practice — and in many cases the legally required one under government resolutions — is to issue a public notice inviting Request for Proposals (RFPs) from developers, setting out the society's requirements: the minimum area to be offered to each member, the corpus fund, the transit rent, the construction timeline, the bank guarantee, and any other key terms. Multiple RFPs should be received, evaluated, and compared on objectively stated criteria, and the selection decision should be documented with reasons.

Accepting the offer of the first developer to approach the society, or selecting a developer based on informal lobbying by influential members, exposes the society to legal challenge and practical risk. A developer whose track record was not verified, whose financial capacity was not assessed, and whose offer was not compared against alternatives may turn out to be incapable of completing the project — leaving members in transit accommodation for years beyond the promised timeline. The self-redevelopment option — where the society borrows construction finance from a co-operative or housing finance institution and manages the construction itself — has gained popularity as a way to capture the full development benefit without sharing it with a private developer, though it requires more active management by the committee and members.

## The Development Agreement: Key Clauses to Negotiate

The development agreement is the single most important document in a redevelopment transaction. It governs everything — what the developer must build, when they must complete it, what each member receives, and what happens if the developer defaults. Key clauses that members must scrutinise include the carpet area guarantee, which specifies the minimum area in the new building that each member is entitled to receive and should be stated in terms of RERA-defined carpet area, not saleable area; the corpus fund quantum and the mechanism for its deposit and safeguarding; transit rent — the monthly amount the developer pays members to cover rent during the construction period — including the rate, the duration, and any escalation provisions for delays beyond the scheduled timeline.

The construction timeline should specify not just a final completion date but intermediate milestones — completion of piling, completion of slab for each floor — with consequences attached to each missed milestone. A bank guarantee from the developer, typically for a percentage of the construction cost or the total transit rent liability, provides members with a financial backstop if the developer defaults or becomes insolvent mid-project. The development agreement should also specify what happens if the developer is replaced mid-project, whether and how members can seek termination if milestones are consistently missed, and the mechanism for resolving disputes — whether through arbitration or the Co-operative Court.

## Member Rights During Redevelopment

Individual members have rights that persist throughout the redevelopment process and that cannot be abridged by a majority decision of the society or by the development agreement. These include the right to receive the agreed carpet area in the new building, to receive transit rent from the date of vacation until possession of the new flat, to inspect the project accounts and the escrow mechanism, and to receive notice of and participate in any material variation to the sanctioned plan or the development agreement that affects their entitlement. If the developer proposes to reduce the offered area, change the flat layout, or alter the amenities promised to members, the affected members' consent is required — a unilateral change imposed by the developer or approved only by the managing committee without a full member resolution is contestable.

## RERA Applicability

Once a redevelopment project crosses the applicable RERA threshold — plot area exceeding 500 square metres or more than eight units — it must be registered with MahaRERA. RERA registration is beneficial for members: it requires the developer to disclose the project timeline, the sanctioned plan, the approvals obtained, and the escrow arrangement under which a prescribed percentage of sale proceeds from new units must be used only for the construction of the project. Members should verify that the developer has obtained RERA registration and should check the project's MahaRERA page regularly for updates on approvals and declared timelines.

## Common Disputes and How to Avoid Them

The most frequent dispute in redevelopment projects involves the developer reducing the carpet area offered to members from what was promised in the development agreement — often justified by changes in the approved plan or the FSI computation. Members should insist that the development agreement specifies the carpet area as a fixed quantum, not as a proportion of the total FSI, and that any reduction below the guaranteed area triggers a penalty or compensation mechanism. Delays in transit rent payment are similarly common and often signal the developer's financial difficulties — the development agreement should require transit rent to be deposited in advance for each quarter, not paid monthly in arrears.

Mid-project change of developer is one of the most disruptive events that can occur in a redevelopment. The development agreement and the society's legal advisors should ensure that clear step-in rights exist — mechanisms by which the society can take over the incomplete project, assign it to an alternative developer, or activate the bank guarantee — without requiring lengthy litigation before the Co-operative Court. Disputes about flat allocation in the new building, particularly regarding which member gets which unit, should be resolved by a transparent lot-based allocation process documented before construction begins rather than negotiated in an ad hoc manner after the building is ready for possession.

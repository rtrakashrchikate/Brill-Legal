The right to a refund under the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act 2016 is one of the most significant protections the legislation confers on homebuyers. Before RERA, a buyer trapped in a stalled or significantly delayed project had limited remedies — consumer forum proceedings could be slow, and contractual agreements often contained clauses that impeded refund claims. Section 18 of the RERA Act changed this by giving buyers a clear statutory right to exit and recover their investment with interest in specified circumstances.

## What Triggers the Right to a Refund Under Section 18

The primary trigger for a Section 18 refund right is the promoter's failure to complete the project or to hand over possession of the unit by the date agreed in the registered agreement for sale. This is the most commonly invoked ground and does not require proof of fault — a delay beyond the contractual date is sufficient, subject to valid force majeure extensions.

Other triggers that courts and RERA authorities have recognised as supporting a refund claim include:

- **Material misrepresentation** about the project — including false statements about approvals, amenities, carpet area, or project specifications that materially influenced the allottee's decision to purchase
- **Project cancellation** — if the promoter's registration is revoked by the RERA authority or the promoter voluntarily abandons the project
- **Significant deviation from sanctioned plans** — construction of the unit in a manner substantially different from what was agreed, including changes to carpet area beyond the 3% tolerance permitted under Section 14 of the RERA Act

## The Quantum of Refund: What You Get Back

Under Section 18, the refund entitlement is the entire amount paid by the allottee to the promoter — covering the booking amount, all instalment payments, and any charges such as preferential location charges (PLC), parking charges, or other amounts paid in connection with the flat — together with interest at the prescribed rate from the date each payment was made until the date of actual refund.

The prescribed interest rate is the SBI's Marginal Cost of Funds based Lending Rate (MCLR) plus 2 percentage points. Practically, this means the total amount recoverable on a refund order is substantially more than the principal — buyers who have held their investment for several years and made payments progressively over that period can expect to recover significantly more than they paid, once interest is calculated.

Compensation for other losses — such as rent paid while waiting for possession, or costs incurred for interior design that was never used — can also be claimed, but such claims are adjudicated separately and generally require evidence of actual loss.

## Section 19: The Allottee's Obligations

Section 18 operates in the context of Section 19, which imposes obligations on allottees, including the obligation to make payments as per the schedule in the agreement for sale. A promoter defending a refund complaint will often point to the allottee's own default in payment — arguing that the delay in construction was caused or contributed to by non-payment of instalments. While this argument does not defeat a Section 18 claim entirely, it can affect the calculation period and, in some cases, the amount of compensation. Allottees who have been in arrears on their payment schedules should be prepared to address this in the complaint.

## Staying in the Project Versus Exiting: The Strategic Choice

The choice between claiming a refund and continuing with the project is not always straightforward. Several factors are relevant:

**Arguments for claiming a refund:**
- The project is substantially incomplete and completion timelines are uncertain
- The promoter appears to be in financial difficulty and future completion is in doubt
- The market value of the flat has declined below the contracted price
- The allottee no longer needs the property for the original purpose (residence or investment)

**Arguments for staying in and claiming interest:**
- The project is near completion and possession is genuinely imminent
- The flat has appreciated significantly in value since the agreement
- A refund would expose the allottee to reinvestment risk in a market where comparable property is more expensive
- The allottee has tax or loan considerations that make cancellation disadvantageous

It is worth noting that the choice of remedy is made at the time of filing the complaint, though RERA authorities have in some cases allowed allottees to amend their prayer from interest to refund if the project situation deteriorates further. Legal advice before filing is helpful to frame the complaint appropriately.

## Tax Implications of a RERA Refund

The return of principal — the amounts originally paid for the flat — is not income and does not attract income tax. However, interest awarded under a RERA order is taxable as income from other sources in the year of receipt. Depending on the quantum, this can be a significant tax liability in the year the order is complied with.

Where the agreement for sale has been subsisting for more than two years, its cancellation may also trigger capital loss implications, particularly if property values have changed. The interaction of RERA refunds with home loan tax benefits (deductions under Section 24 and 80C of the Income Tax Act that were claimed during the period the loan was active) also requires review. A chartered accountant should be consulted before a refund is finalised.

## Recovery After a MahaRERA Refund Order

An order directing a promoter to refund the amount paid with interest is enforceable as a decree of a civil court. The allottee has several recovery mechanisms available:

- Application to MahaRERA for execution proceedings against the promoter's assets
- Recovery through the District Collector as arrears of land revenue — a particularly effective mechanism since it does not require a separate court application
- Civil court execution petition

In cases where the promoter is insolvent or has multiple creditors, homebuyers who are financial creditors under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016 may file an application before the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) seeking insolvency resolution or liquidation. The IBC route is separate from RERA and has its own procedural requirements, but it has been successfully used by organised groups of homebuyers to recover amounts from stalled real estate companies.

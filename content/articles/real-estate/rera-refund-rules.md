Before RERA, a homebuyer who wanted to exit a delayed or misrepresented project had limited options — a consumer complaint that could take years, a civil suit for breach of contract, or absorbing the loss and waiting. The seller-favoured agreements of the pre-RERA era often included cancellation clauses that returned only a fraction of the purchase price, with forfeiture of booking amounts and processing fees, giving the promoter a strong advantage in any dispute. RERA changed the calculus fundamentally. Section 18 of the RERA Act establishes a statutory right to exit a project and claim a full refund with interest — a right that cannot be waived or contracted away by the parties, and that does not require the buyer to prove fraud or bad faith, only delay or misrepresentation.

## Triggers for Refund Eligibility

The right to a refund under RERA can arise from several distinct triggers, each addressed in the statutory framework. The most common is delay in possession under Section 18(1): if the promoter fails to complete construction or hand over possession by the date specified in the agreement for sale, the allottee who chooses to withdraw is entitled to a full refund of every amount paid, with interest from the date of each payment and compensation for any additional loss suffered. This trigger does not require the allottee to show that the project has been abandoned — even a single day of delay beyond the contractual date is technically sufficient, though in practice MahaRERA often considers the full picture of project progress.

The second major trigger is material misrepresentation or false information provided in the prospectus, brochure, advertisement, or any other material, under Section 18(3). If a promoter promised certain specifications, amenities, or features in the sales material and then failed to deliver them — a different layout, a missing clubhouse, an altered view corridor, a reduced carpet area — the allottee may claim compensation for the loss caused by that misrepresentation. In severe cases where the misrepresentation goes to the fundamental character of the unit, an exit and refund order is possible. Structural defects discovered within five years of possession that the promoter refuses to rectify can also ground a complaint, though the refund remedy in defect cases is less straightforward than in pure delay cases.

Finally, where a project's RERA registration is revoked by the authority — because the promoter failed to complete the project, failed to maintain the escrow, or committed serious violations — the allottees are entitled to seek refunds. This is the most extreme outcome, and one that MahaRERA typically accompanies with orders against the promoter's assets to fund the return of allottee money.

## Section 19: Buyer Obligations

A frequently overlooked aspect of RERA's refund framework is that it is not a one-way protection. Section 19 imposes obligations on allottees as well: they must make payments on the schedule specified in the registered agreement for sale, pay interest for any delayed payments, and take possession within two months of receiving an offer of possession (once the occupancy certificate has been issued). An allottee who is in material default of their own payment obligations under the agreement — having failed to pay instalments on time without adequate justification — may find their Section 18 claim weakened, since the promoter may argue that the delay was partly caused or contributed to by the allottee's own default. Maintaining a clean payment record, and preserving evidence of every payment made on time, is therefore important for any allottee who may later need to invoke Section 18 rights.

## The Choice: Exit vs. Stay and Claim Interest

The most consequential decision a delayed allottee faces is whether to exercise the exit option under Section 18(1) or to stay in the project and claim running interest for the period of delay. There is no single right answer — it depends on the project's stage of completion, the promoter's financial stability, the buyer's own financial position and loan commitments, and the buyer's assessment of how long further delay is likely.

Exiting makes the strongest sense where the project is significantly stalled with no realistic completion timeline in sight, where the promoter shows signs of financial distress or has stopped communicating, or where the buyer's circumstances have changed materially and they no longer need or want the property. Staying and claiming interest makes more sense where the project is near completion — perhaps sixty to eighty percent constructed — and the promoter is actively working on site, because in that scenario exit and refund may generate less value than the completed flat once possession is eventually taken. The interest claim compensates the buyer for the time value of money during the delay period without requiring them to find an alternative property.

## Quantum of Refund: What You Get Back

Under Section 18(1), the exiting allottee is entitled to the full amount paid — every instalment, booking amount, and other charge paid to the promoter under the agreement — plus interest at the prescribed rate (SBI MCLR plus 2%, compounded monthly under the Maharashtra RERA Rules) from the date of each individual payment until the date of actual refund. This is a more generous formulation than many buyers realise: the interest is not just from the agreed possession date, but from the date each payment was actually made, acknowledging that the allottee has been deprived of the use of that money since the moment they paid it.

Importantly, the refund is the amount paid — not the current market value of the property. If the project is in a locality where property values have risen substantially since the buyer signed the agreement, the refund does not capture that appreciation. In a rising market, an allottee who exits under Section 18 may therefore receive less in economic terms than they would by waiting for possession. Conversely, in a falling market or where the project is in distress, the statutory refund with interest may represent a better economic outcome than the finished flat.

## Tax Implications of the Refund

The principal amount returned — the original purchase price paid — is not income and is generally not taxable in the hands of the allottee. However, the interest component of the RERA refund — the interest awarded for delay under Section 18 — is taxable as income from other sources under Section 56 of the Income Tax Act and must be declared in the year of receipt. Where the buyer had previously claimed a home loan interest deduction under Section 24 of the Income Tax Act and the principal repayment deduction under Section 80C, those deductions may be required to be reversed upon refund, since the underlying purchase transaction no longer exists. The tax implications of a RERA refund can be material in a large transaction, and consulting a tax advisor on the specific facts before filing the complaint is advisable.

## After the Order: Enforcement

MahaRERA orders directing refund are enforceable as decrees of a civil court under Section 40 of the RERA Act. Where the promoter does not comply within the time specified in the order, the allottee may file an execution petition before the MahaRERA authority or a competent court. MahaRERA has the power to attach and sell the promoter's assets — including unsold units in the very project — to satisfy outstanding orders. In practice, execution proceedings can take additional time, particularly where the promoter has limited liquid assets and the unsold inventory must be sold to realise the refund amount. In cases of persistent non-compliance, MahaRERA may also initiate action against the promoter's registration, which is a significant lever given that a de-registered promoter cannot sell or market units in any project.

## Home Loan Considerations

Allottees who financed their purchase through a home loan must plan carefully before filing for a RERA refund. The lender — whether a bank or a housing finance company — has a charge on the refund proceeds, and the refund order typically directs that the lender be paid first from the amount refunded. The allottee must inform their lender when a RERA complaint is filed so that the lender can note its interest in the proceedings and co-ordinate on the application of the refund amount to the outstanding loan balance. Any prepayment charges under the loan agreement will be the allottee's responsibility and should be factored into the economic calculation of whether to exit.
